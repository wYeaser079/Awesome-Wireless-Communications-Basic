PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

At the end of Section II we had: every AP owns an MMSE estimate ĝ_mk of its
channel to every user. Now we have to USE those estimates to actually
transmit data. Section III studies the simplest possible way: CONJUGATE
BEAMFORMING (CB), also called Maximum-Ratio Transmission (MRT).

IDEA IN ONE SENTENCE. To send symbol s_i to user i, AP m multiplies s_i by
the COMPLEX CONJUGATE of its estimated channel to user i, scaled by a knob
√(η_mi) it can turn up or down, then sums these contributions for all users.

WHY CONJUGATE? Because of the following elementary fact. If you want the
signal you transmit, after going through the channel g_mk, to appear
COHERENTLY (in phase) at user k, you should pre-twist its phase by the
opposite amount. Multiplying by ĝ*_mk (conjugate of the estimate) exactly
pre-twists the phase so that when the signal passes through g_mk ≈ ĝ_mk,
the two phases cancel and you get a positive real number — the amplitude
adds constructively. If M APs all do this simultaneously, their M separate
contributions line up in phase at user k and add up, giving a coherent
power gain that grows with M (this is the classical "beamforming gain").

The catch is that the SAME transmission x_m also reaches users OTHER THAN
k. Their phases don't align. But on average, because h_mk are i.i.d. across
k, the interference to user k from signals intended for user j ≠ k looks
roughly like noise. This is called MULTI-USER INTERFERENCE (MUI) and is
exactly what CB does not cancel (unlike ZF which cancels it explicitly —
that's in Section IV).

The key design variables are the K·M power coefficients η_mk — one knob
per (AP, user) pair. The central question of Section III-A is: how do you
set all these knobs so that the WORST user gets the best possible SINR,
subject to the physical constraint that each AP has a max transmit power?

That's a max-min optimization problem. Unfortunately, it is NOT convex in
its raw form — the SINR numerator is a squared sum of square roots, which
is quadratic and concave but not linear. It turns out to be QUASI-CONCAVE
(meaning: the super-level sets {η : SINR_k(η) ≥ t} are convex for each t).
Quasi-concave problems are solved by BISECTION — you binary-search over
the SINR target t, and at each trial value you ask "is it feasible to
achieve SINR ≥ t for everyone?" That feasibility test is a convex problem
(specifically, a second-order cone program), which is cheap. After ~20
bisection steps you have the optimum to within 2⁻²⁰ ≈ 10⁻⁶ relative error.

The price is complexity: solving a convex SOCP at every bisection step
costs a lot, and the M×K number of variables scales painfully. This
motivates the cheaper heuristic allocations of Sections III-B, III-C, III-D
(treated in the next file).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1  THE CB PROTOCOL (PAGE 3, TOP OF SECTION III)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper states the entire CB operation as a five-step protocol:

  Step 1. AP m estimates β_mk for k=1,…,K (long-term averaging of pilot
          powers) and sends these slow-varying scalars to the NC.
  Step 2. NC computes power coefficients η_mk ∀m, ∀k, as a function of
          all the β_mk's, and sends them back to the APs.
  Step 3. Users synchronously transmit pilot sequences ψ_i, i=1,…,K.
  Step 4. AP m computes its own channel estimates ĝ_mk, k=1,…,K.
  Step 5. AP m forms the CB transmit signal

             x_m = √(ρ_f) · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ √(η_mi) · ĝ*_mi · s_i              (3)

          and transmits it over the downlink.

Three features stand out:

(a) THE η_mk ARE FUNCTIONS OF β_mk ONLY. This is a design decision — η_mk
    depends only on LARGE-SCALE fading, not on small-scale fading. Why?
    Because the capacity lower bound in Eq. (5) depends only on β_mk and
    γ_mk, both of which are derivable from β_mk alone. Since β changes
    ~40× slower than h, the η-update traffic on the backhaul is minimal.

(b) CONJUGATE BEAMFORMING IS DISTRIBUTED. Once the NC sends η_mk, each AP
    m can form x_m independently using only its OWN estimates ĝ_m1,…,ĝ_mK.
    Contrast with ZF (Section IV), which requires the NC to invert the
    full Ĝᵀ Ĝ matrix and to send M×K scalar precoding coefficients back
    to the APs — far heavier backhaul.

(c) PER-AP POWER IS THE BINDING CONSTRAINT, NOT TOTAL POWER. The rationale
    is hardware-centric: each AP has its own power amplifier whose peak
    output is ρ_f (e.g., 200 mW). You cannot "borrow power" from an
    idle AP. This restricts the feasible set.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  PER-AP POWER CONSTRAINT: derivation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The expected transmit power of AP m is:

       E[|x_m|²]  =  E[ | √(ρ_f) · Σᵢ √(η_mi) · ĝ*_mi · s_i |² ]
                  =  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σⱼ √(η_mi η_mj) · E[ĝ*_mi ĝ_mj] · E[s_i s*_j]
                  =  ρ_f · Σᵢ η_mi · E[|ĝ_mi|²]       (since E[s_is*_j]=δ_{ij},
                                                         E[ĝ*_mi ĝ_mj]=0 for i≠j)
                  =  ρ_f · Σᵢ η_mi · γ_mi.                          (A)

The per-AP power constraint E[|x_m|²] ≤ ρ_f becomes:

       Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_mi · γ_mi  ≤  1,    ∀m ∈ {1,…,M}.                   (6b)

This is the "budget rule" at AP m: the weighted sum of its power
coefficients (weighted by the estimate-variances γ_mi) cannot exceed 1.

Three observations:
  • The weights are γ_mi, NOT β_mi. So APs with a better channel ESTIMATE
    to user i (not just a better channel) "spend" more of their budget
    per unit η_mi.
  • The constraint COUPLES the K power coefficients at AP m, but NOT
    across APs. Different APs have independent budgets.
  • When η_mi = 0 for all i (AP m is silent), the left side is 0 ≤ 1 —
    always feasible. AP silence is always admissible.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3  THE ACHIEVABLE RATE FORMULA — EQUATION (5)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper states the CB achievable rate as:

       R_k  =  log₂( 1 + SINR_k )                                    (5a)

where

                 ρ_f · ( Σₘ₌₁ᴹ √(η_mk) · γ_mk )²
       SINR_k = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────   (5)
                 1  +  ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ Σₘ₌₁ᴹ η_mi · β_mk · γ_mi

Structure:

  NUMERATOR — the "useful signal power":
      ρ_f · (Σₘ √(η_mk) γ_mk)². The squared-sum-of-square-roots form is
      the signature of COHERENT combination: contributions from different
      APs to user k add in amplitude (not power), then get squared. This
      gives a gain that scales roughly as M² — "array gain".

  DENOMINATOR — noise (the "1") plus residual interference:
      ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi · β_mk · γ_mi.

      Note two weights: β_mk (how strongly user k actually hears AP m's
      signal) × γ_mi (the energy AP m spends on user i). This is the
      INCOHERENT sum, scaling roughly as M (not M²).

The ratio of array gains — numerator M² vs. denominator M — means SINR
grows linearly with M. This is the Massive MIMO "free lunch": just adding
more APs improves SINR for everyone, without any algorithmic changes.

Why is (5) a function of β and γ only (no h_mk)? Because it is an
EXPECTED SINR formula derived via the worst-case uncorrelated-noise
argument, and the expectation averages out all small-scale fading. The
user's receiver treats the random effective gain as deterministic, relying
on channel hardening (Massive MIMO concentration) to justify this.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  DERIVATION OF SINR_k (Appendix A)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is one of the most important derivations of the paper. We'll do it
carefully because the same "T₀–T₃ decomposition" reappears verbatim for
ZF in Appendix C.

STARTING POINT. User k's received signal (Eq. 4 with x_m from Eq. 3):

   y_k  =  Σₘ g_mk · x_m  +  w_k
        =  Σₘ g_mk · [ √(ρ_f) · Σᵢ √(η_mi) · ĝ*_mi · s_i ]  +  w_k
        =  √(ρ_f) · Σₘ Σᵢ √(η_mi) · g_mk · ĝ*_mi · s_i  +  w_k.

Now decompose g_mk = ĝ_mk + ~g_mk (estimate + estimation error):

   y_k  =  √(ρ_f) · Σₘ Σᵢ √(η_mi) · (ĝ_mk + ~g_mk) · ĝ*_mi · s_i  +  w_k.

Expand and split into the desired-symbol (i=k) term and the
other-symbols (i≠k) term, further decomposing the diagonal piece into its
mean and fluctuation:

   y_k  =  T₀  +  T₁  +  T₂  +  T₃  +  w_k,                           (30)

where:

  T₀  =  Σₘ √(ρ_f η_mk) · E[|ĝ_mk|²] · s_k
       ←  "useful signal": the part that is deterministic in the estimate
           statistics, carrying the symbol s_k.

  T₁  =  Σₘ Σᵢ≠k √(ρ_f η_mi) · ĝ_mk · ĝ*_mi · s_i
       ←  "inter-user interference": when AP m beamforms toward user i≠k,
           its precoded signal also hits user k with cross-product ĝ_mk · ĝ*_mi.
           This is residual MUI because CB does not cancel it.

  T₂  =  Σₘ √(ρ_f η_mk) · ( |ĝ_mk|² − E[|ĝ_mk|²] ) · s_k
       ←  "lack of channel knowledge at user": user k doesn't know
           |ĝ_mk|², only its mean γ_mk. The fluctuation around the mean
           becomes self-interference.

  T₃  =  Σₘ Σᵢ √(ρ_f η_mi) · ~g_mk · ĝ*_mi · s_i
       ←  "channel estimation error": the AP's precoder uses ĝ_mi, but the
           actual channel contains an unknown ~g_mk component, which leaks
           through and creates interference.

  w_k  =  receiver noise.

Key claim: T₀, T₁, T₂, T₃, w_k are MUTUALLY UNCORRELATED.
Why?
  • Data symbols s_i are independent, zero-mean, unit-power; any two
    terms carrying different s_i are uncorrelated.
  • T₀ and T₂ both carry s_k but T₂ is a fluctuation (zero-mean) orthogonal
    to the constant mean in T₀, so E[T₀ · T₂*] = 0.
  • ĝ_mk and ~g_mk are independent (Section II orthogonality principle),
    so terms involving them separately are uncorrelated.
  • Noise w_k is independent of everything.

This is the precondition for applying the HASSIBI–HOCHWALD BOUND [30]:

    "If y = T₀·s + z where T₀·s is the desired component and z is
     uncorrelated with s, then an achievable rate is
         log₂(1 + E[|T₀|²] / E[|z|²]),
     obtained by treating z as Gaussian with the same second moment."

Applied here with z = T₁ + T₂ + T₃ + w_k:

                   E[|T₀|²]
    SINR_k  =  ─────────────────────────────────                    (31)
                  1 + E[|T₁|² + |T₂|² + |T₃|²]
                      (the 1 is Var(w_k))

Now compute each variance.

VARIANCE OF T₀.
   T₀ = ( Σₘ √(ρ_f η_mk) · γ_mk ) · s_k      (since E[|ĝ_mk|²]=γ_mk)
   |T₀|² = ρ_f · ( Σₘ √(η_mk) γ_mk )² · |s_k|².
   Since E[|s_k|²] = 1:

       E[|T₀|²]  =  ρ_f · ( Σₘ₌₁ᴹ √(η_mk) · γ_mk )².                 (T0)

VARIANCE OF T₁.
   T₁ = Σₘ Σᵢ≠k √(ρ_f η_mi) · ĝ_mk · ĝ*_mi · s_i.
   For i ≠ j, E[s_i s*_j] = 0, so cross terms vanish. For i = j ≠ k:

       E[|T₁|²]  =  Σᵢ≠k ρ_f · Σₘ Σₙ √(η_mi η_ni) · E[ĝ_mk ĝ*_mi · ĝ*_nk ĝ_ni].

   Since ĝ_mk is independent of ĝ_nk for m ≠ n (AP antennas are spatially
   separated, h_mk i.i.d.), and since ĝ_mk ⊥ ĝ_mi for i ≠ k (pilots
   orthogonal), only the m = n terms survive:

       E[|T₁|²]  =  Σᵢ≠k ρ_f · Σₘ η_mi · E[|ĝ_mk|² · |ĝ_mi|²]
                 =  Σᵢ≠k ρ_f · Σₘ η_mi · γ_mk · γ_mi                (indep)
                 =  ρ_f · Σᵢ≠k Σₘ η_mi · γ_mk · γ_mi.                (T1)

VARIANCE OF T₂.
   T₂ = Σₘ √(ρ_f η_mk) · ( |ĝ_mk|² − γ_mk ) · s_k.
   Since |ĝ_mk|² is the squared magnitude of a 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk), it is
   exponential with mean γ_mk and variance γ_mk². So:

       E[|T₂|²]  =  Σₘ ρ_f · η_mk · Var(|ĝ_mk|²)
                 =  Σₘ ρ_f · η_mk · γ_mk²
                 =  ρ_f · Σₘ η_mk · γ²_mk.                          (T2)

   (Cross terms for m ≠ n vanish due to independence.)

VARIANCE OF T₃.
   T₃ = Σₘ Σᵢ √(ρ_f η_mi) · ~g_mk · ĝ*_mi · s_i.
   Same logic: E[s_i s*_j] = 0 for i ≠ j; ~g_mk ⊥ ĝ_mi; ~g_mk ⊥ ~g_nk for
   m ≠ n. Only diagonal (m=n) terms survive:

       E[|T₃|²]  =  Σᵢ ρ_f · Σₘ η_mi · E[|~g_mk|²] · E[|ĝ_mi|²]
                 =  Σᵢ ρ_f · Σₘ η_mi · (β_mk − γ_mk) · γ_mi
                 =  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi · (β_mk − γ_mk) · γ_mi.        (T3)

COMBINING T₁, T₂, T₃:

       E[|T₁|² + |T₂|² + |T₃|²]
     =  ρ_f · Σᵢ≠k Σₘ η_mi γ_mk γ_mi
      + ρ_f · Σₘ η_mk γ²_mk
      + ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi (β_mk − γ_mk) γ_mi

Notice that the first two terms together are ρ_f Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi γ_mk γ_mi
(because the i=k piece missing from T₁ is supplied by T₂ with
γ²_mk = γ_mk · γ_mk).

So:
     =  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi γ_mk γ_mi  +  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi (β_mk − γ_mk) γ_mi
     =  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi [γ_mk + (β_mk − γ_mk)] γ_mi
     =  ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi · β_mk · γ_mi.                            (denom)

The total denominator of SINR_k is 1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi β_mk γ_mi, which
matches Eq. (5). ✓

This is why Eq. (5) has β_mk · γ_mi in the interference term rather than
just β_mk · β_mi — the factor γ_mi (< β_mi) reflects the fact that only
the ESTIMATED part of the channel is actually beamformed; the
estimation-error part still leaks but contributes via (β_mk − γ_mk) · γ_mi
rather than via β_mk · β_mi. The two combine neatly to β_mk · γ_mi.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.5  THE MAX-MIN POWER ALLOCATION PROBLEM (Eq. 6)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Stacking all K·M power coefficients into a vector
   η = (η_mk : m ∈ {1,…,M}, k ∈ {1,…,K}) ∈ ℝ_{+}^{MK},
the problem is:

   maximize_η     min_{k∈{1,…,K}}   SINR_k(η)                       (6a)

   subject to     Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_mi · γ_mi  ≤  1,   ∀m ∈ {1,…,M}.          (6b)

This is our max-min power control problem. It has three important
properties:

(i) NON-NEGATIVITY. η_mk ≥ 0 is implicit (they are squared quantities).

(ii) DECOUPLED CONSTRAINTS ACROSS APs. For each AP m, the constraint is a
     simple linear inequality in the variables {η_m1,…,η_mK}.

(iii) OBJECTIVE IS QUASI-CONCAVE. The minimum over k of SINR_k is
      quasi-concave, because each SINR_k is quasi-concave in η and the
      min of quasi-concave functions is quasi-concave. The paper cites
      [22, Proposition 1] for this. The proof relies on showing that for
      any t > 0, the super-level set {η : SINR_k(η) ≥ t} is convex.

WHY DOES SINR_k HAVE A CONVEX SUPER-LEVEL SET?
Rewrite the constraint SINR_k(η) ≥ t:

   ρ_f · ( Σₘ √(η_mk) γ_mk )²  ≥  t · ( 1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi β_mk γ_mi ).

Take square roots (both sides are non-negative):

   √(ρ_f) · Σₘ √(η_mk) γ_mk   ≥   √t · √( 1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ Σₘ η_mi β_mk γ_mi ).

Substitute u_mk = √(η_mk). Then η_mk = u²_mk. The LHS becomes linear in
u_mk:
   LHS = √(ρ_f) · Σₘ γ_mk · u_mk.

The RHS becomes the 2-norm of an affine function of u:
   RHS = √t · ‖ (1, √(ρ_f · β_mk · γ_mi) · u_mi for all m, i) ‖₂.

This is a second-order cone (SOC) constraint:
   (linear in u)  ≥  ‖ (constant + affine-in-u) ‖₂.

SOC constraints are convex, and the intersection over k=1,…,K is convex.
The per-AP power constraint Σᵢ u²_mi γ_mi ≤ 1 is also convex (quadratic
with positive semidefinite diagonal). So the FEASIBILITY SET for fixed t
is convex. Quasi-concavity is established.

This justifies the bisection method (Eq. 7 in the paper).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  THE BISECTION METHOD (Eq. 7)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reformulated problem:

   maximize_{η, t}   t                                             (7a)
   s.t.   SINR_k(η) ≥ t   for k=1,…,K,
          Σᵢ η_mi γ_mi ≤ 1   for m=1,…,M.                          (7b)

BISECTION ALGORITHM (standard, Boyd & Vandenberghe §4.2.5 [26]):

  Input: an initial interval (t_ℓ, t_u) known to contain t*.
         For example, t_ℓ = 0, t_u = a crude SINR upper bound derived
         by ignoring interference.
  Tolerance ε > 0.

  while (t_u − t_ℓ) > ε:
      t  ←  (t_ℓ + t_u) / 2
      Solve the convex FEASIBILITY problem:
          Find η ≥ 0
          s.t.  √(ρ_f) · Σₘ γ_mk · u_mk
                    ≥ √t · ‖ (1, √(ρ_f β_mk γ_mi)·u_mi ∀m,i) ‖₂    ∀k
                Σᵢ η_mi γ_mi ≤ 1    ∀m
          (with u_mk = √η_mk).
      if feasible:
          t_ℓ ← t               # can push target higher
          save current η as the best so far
      else:
          t_u ← t               # target was too high

  return (η_best, t_ℓ)

Each feasibility step is a second-order cone feasibility problem (SOCP)
with:
  • K second-order cone constraints (one per user),
  • M linear constraints (one per AP),
  • MK scalar variables.

Interior-point methods solve SOCPs in polynomial time. The practical cost
per iteration is O((MK)³·(K+M)) roughly, though more careful analyses
tighten this. With bisection's log₂(1/ε) outer iterations, total cost is
polynomial in M, K, and log(1/ε).

This is prohibitive for large M and K (say M = 256, K = 32). Hence the
heuristics in Sec. III-B/C/D — addressed in the next file.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  INITIAL INTERVAL (t_ℓ, t_u) — A PRACTICAL REMARK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper does not explicitly give (t_ℓ, t_u), but a natural choice:

  t_ℓ = 0  (trivially feasible, η = 0).

  t_u = min_k of the "noise-free, single-user" SINR, which corresponds to
        allocating all of AP m's budget entirely to user k:
           t_u  ≈  min_k  ρ_f · [ Σₘ γ_mk / (max_AP power weight) ]²
        A conservative upper bound is:
           t_u  ≈  ρ_f · (Σₘ γ_mk)² / (normalization).

In practice, one starts with (0, a very large number) and lets bisection
tighten.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1  MAXIMUM RATIO TRANSMISSION (MRT) = CONJUGATE BEAMFORMING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The name "conjugate beamforming" is a Massive MIMO rebranding of what
earlier literature called MAXIMUM RATIO TRANSMISSION (MRT). MRT was
introduced for transmit diversity in:

  • T. K. Y. Lo, "Maximum ratio transmission," IEEE Trans. Commun.,
    vol. 47, no. 10, pp. 1458–1461, Oct. 1999.

Lo showed that if a multi-antenna transmitter knows the channel vector g
to a single-antenna receiver, the precoding vector that maximizes received
SNR subject to a transmit-power constraint is g*/‖g‖ — the conjugate of
the channel, normalized. This "steers all power toward the dominant
propagation direction" and gives the ARRAY GAIN of M.

In a multi-user setting, each data stream is CB-precoded toward its
intended user. The residual MUI is the price paid for not doing ZF.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2  CHANNEL HARDENING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The assumption that users know only E[|ĝ_mk|²] = γ_mk (not the instantaneous
value) works because of channel hardening — a concentration phenomenon
first exploited in Massive MIMO by Marzetta [2] and later analyzed more
deeply by Hochwald, Marzetta, Tarokh in the monograph by Marzetta, Larsson,
Yang, Ngo, "Fundamentals of Massive MIMO" (Cambridge, 2016), and by
Björnson et al.

The essence: if you form the quantity (1/M) Σₘ |ĝ_mk|² γ_mk, the Law of
Large Numbers says it converges to a deterministic constant as M grows.
So the effective channel gain at user k stops fluctuating, and the user
can use the deterministic bound (5) as a tight approximation of the true
capacity. The difference is small in practice (the paper cites [22, Fig. 2]
which shows it).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  HASSIBI–HOCHWALD WORST-CASE UNCORRELATED NOISE ARGUMENT [30]
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Given a signal
   y = x + z,
where x is the desired signal and z is zero-mean and uncorrelated with x
(but not necessarily Gaussian), the MUTUAL INFORMATION I(x; y) is
LOWER-BOUNDED by treating z as Gaussian with the same variance:

   I(x; y)  ≥  log(1 + Var(x) / Var(z)).

Proof sketch: among all zero-mean, fixed-variance distributions, Gaussian
noise has MAXIMUM differential entropy, hence yields the MINIMUM mutual
information when noise is replaced. Since I(x;y) = h(y) − h(z), replacing
z by Gaussian only decreases h(z)'s entropy in the bound — wait, this
needs more care. Actually the correct statement: treating z as Gaussian
yields the WORST possible noise for communication given its
autocorrelation, so it gives a LOWER BOUND on achievable rate. This is
exactly Theorem 1 of Hassibi–Hochwald 2003 [30].

This is the hammer used everywhere in Massive MIMO achievable-rate analysis.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.4  QUASI-CONCAVITY OF THE CB SINR — CONNECTION TO GEOMETRIC PROGRAMMING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Maximization of minimum SINR for linear precoders in multi-user MIMO has a
long history. Key references:

  • M. Bengtsson and B. Ottersten, "Optimal and suboptimal transmit
    beamforming," in Handbook of Antennas in Wireless Communications,
    CRC Press, 2001.
    [First to formulate max-min SINR as an SOCP.]

  • M. Schubert and H. Boche, "Solution of the multiuser downlink
    beamforming problem with individual SINR constraints," IEEE Trans.
    Veh. Technol., vol. 53, no. 1, pp. 18–28, Jan. 2004.
    [Max-min SINR via fixed-point iteration.]

  • A. Wiesel, Y. Eldar, S. Shamai, "Linear precoding via conic
    optimization for fixed MIMO receivers," IEEE Trans. Signal Process.,
    vol. 54, no. 1, pp. 161–176, Jan. 2006.
    [Conic formulation for a broad class of precoding problems.]

The specific quasi-concavity result for cell-free CB is in Ngo et al.
[22, Prop. 1]. That proposition shows that each SINR_k(η) is quasi-concave
in η via the same SOC-substitution trick we performed.

Cell-free CB is also related to UPLINK-DOWNLINK DUALITY (Viswanath-Tse
[6]). In multi-user MIMO, the downlink max-min power control can often be
mapped to an equivalent uplink problem via a duality transformation, which
is how Schubert-Boche's fixed-point algorithm works. The paper does not
invoke this directly but the machinery is equivalent.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1  TOY EXAMPLE: M = 2, K = 2
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a minimal example to see the structure of (5) concretely.

Setup:
   M = 2 APs, K = 2 users.
   β = [[β_{1,1}, β_{1,2}], [β_{2,1}, β_{2,2}]] = [[10, 1], [1, 10]] · 10⁻⁸.
   (AP 1 is close to user 1; AP 2 is close to user 2; cross-links are weak.)

   Assume γ_mk = β_mk (perfect estimation, for simplicity).

   ρ_f = 1 (normalized).

The CB SINR for user 1 is:

   SINR_1 = ρ_f · ( √η_{1,1} γ_{1,1} + √η_{2,1} γ_{2,1} )² /
             (1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ (η_{1,i} β_{1,1} γ_{1,i} + η_{2,i} β_{2,1} γ_{2,i}))

With symbolic substitution:
   Numerator = ( √η_{1,1} · 10·10⁻⁸  +  √η_{2,1} · 1·10⁻⁸ )²
             = 10⁻¹⁶ · ( 10·√η_{1,1} + √η_{2,1} )²

   Denominator (minus the 1):
     AP 1 contribution:   10·10⁻⁸ · [ η_{1,1} · 10·10⁻⁸ + η_{1,2} · 1·10⁻⁸ ]
                        = 10⁻¹⁵ · (10 η_{1,1} + η_{1,2})
     AP 2 contribution:   1·10⁻⁸  · [ η_{2,1} · 1·10⁻⁸ + η_{2,2} · 10·10⁻⁸ ]
                        = 10⁻¹⁶ · (η_{2,1} + 10 η_{2,2})
     Interference total = 10⁻¹⁶ · (100·η_{1,1} + 10·η_{1,2} + η_{2,1} + 10·η_{2,2}).

Per-AP constraints:
   AP 1:  η_{1,1} · γ_{1,1} + η_{1,2} · γ_{1,2}  ≤ 1
          ⟹  η_{1,1} · 10⁻⁷ + η_{1,2} · 10⁻⁸  ≤ 1
          ⟹  10·η_{1,1} + η_{1,2}  ≤  10⁸.
   AP 2:  η_{2,1} + 10·η_{2,2}  ≤  10⁸.

By symmetry, the optimum has η_{1,1} = η_{2,2} = a (stronger link) and
η_{1,2} = η_{2,1} = b (weaker link).

With this symmetry:
   AP 1 constraint: 10a + b ≤ 10⁸.

Let's pick (a, b) = (10⁷, 0) — all power on the near user, nothing on the
far user:
   Numerator for user 1 (with η_{1,1}=10⁷, η_{2,1}=0):
        10⁻¹⁶ · (10 · √(10⁷) )²  =  10⁻¹⁶ · 100 · 10⁷  =  10⁻⁷.
   Denominator contribution for user 1 (η_{1,1}=10⁷, η_{1,2}=0,
                                         η_{2,1}=0, η_{2,2}=10⁷):
        10⁻¹⁶ · (100·10⁷ + 0 + 0 + 10·10⁷)  =  10⁻¹⁶ · 1.1·10⁹  =  1.1·10⁻⁷.
   SINR_1 = 10⁻⁷ / (1 + 1.1·10⁻⁷) ≈ 0.999·10⁻⁷ (tiny; noise dominates
            because we chose ρ_f = 1 with β ~ 10⁻⁸).

Now take (a, b) = (5·10⁶, 5·10⁷) — spread power more:
   AP 1 constraint: 10·(5·10⁶) + 5·10⁷ = 5·10⁷ + 5·10⁷ = 10⁸  ✓ (tight).
   Numerator for user 1:
        10⁻¹⁶ · (10·√(5·10⁶) + √(5·10⁷))²
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · (10·2236 + 7071)²
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · (22360 + 7071)²
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · (29431)²
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · 8.66·10⁸
      ≈ 8.66·10⁻⁸.
   Denominator contribution:
        10⁻¹⁶ · (100·(5·10⁶) + 10·(5·10⁷) + (5·10⁷) + 10·(5·10⁶))
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · (5·10⁸ + 5·10⁸ + 5·10⁷ + 5·10⁷)
      = 10⁻¹⁶ · 1.1·10⁹
      = 1.1·10⁻⁷.
   SINR_1 ≈ 8.66·10⁻⁸ / (1 + 1.1·10⁻⁷) ≈ 8.66·10⁻⁸ — still small, but
   more balanced between user 1 and user 2.

The lesson: in the actual paper's experiments, β values are much larger
(not ~10⁻⁸) after normalizing by noise. The ρ_f · β product should exceed
unity; otherwise SINR is below −10 dB and the system is useless.

Realistic normalized numbers (from paper parameters in Section VI): when
ρ_f/noise ≈ 10¹²·⁵ and β ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ (COST-Hata at a few hundred meters),
the product ρ_f β ≈ 10² — giving SINRs in the tens of dB range.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2  PRACTICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM. Consider a cell-free system with M = 4 APs and K = 2 users. Assume
perfect channel estimation (γ_mk = β_mk). The large-scale fading matrix
(in normalized units, i.e., β̃_mk = ρ_f β_mk) is:

     β̃ = | 100   1   |   (AP 1: very close to user 1)
          |  50   2   |   (AP 2: medium-close to user 1)
          |   2  50   |   (AP 3: medium-close to user 2)
          |   1  100  |   (AP 4: very close to user 2)

Task 1: Write the SINR formula for user 1 explicitly as a function of
        η_{1,1}, η_{2,1}, η_{3,1}, η_{4,1}, η_{1,2}, η_{2,2}, η_{3,2}, η_{4,2}.

Task 2: If power is equally split at each AP (η_{m,1} = η_{m,2} = η_m for
        all m) and each AP is at full power (η_m · (β_{m,1} + β_{m,2}) = 1),
        compute the per-AP power coefficients η_m.

Task 3: Compute SINR_1 and SINR_2 under this symmetric allocation.

SOLUTION.

Task 1. Using (5) with γ = β:

   SINR_1 = ( Σₘ √(η_{m,1}) β_{m,1} )² /
             (1 + Σₘ β_{m,1} · Σᵢ η_{m,i} β_{m,i})

   = ( √η_{1,1}·100 + √η_{2,1}·50 + √η_{3,1}·2 + √η_{4,1}·1 )² /
     (1 + [100·(100·η_{1,1} + 1·η_{1,2}) + 50·(50·η_{2,1} + 2·η_{2,2})
          + 2·(2·η_{3,1} + 50·η_{3,2}) + 1·(1·η_{4,1} + 100·η_{4,2})]).

Task 2. Per-AP full-power under equal splitting:

   AP 1: η_1 · (100 + 1) = 1  ⟹  η_1 = 1/101 ≈ 0.0099.
   AP 2: η_2 · (50 + 2)  = 1  ⟹  η_2 = 1/52  ≈ 0.0192.
   AP 3: η_3 · (2 + 50)  = 1  ⟹  η_3 = 1/52  ≈ 0.0192.
   AP 4: η_4 · (1 + 100) = 1  ⟹  η_4 = 1/101 ≈ 0.0099.

Task 3. Compute SINR_1:

   Numerator:
      (√η_1·100 + √η_2·50 + √η_3·2 + √η_4·1)²
      = (√0.0099·100 + √0.0192·50 + √0.0192·2 + √0.0099·1)²
      = (0.0995·100 + 0.1386·50 + 0.1386·2 + 0.0995·1)²
      = (9.95 + 6.93 + 0.277 + 0.0995)²
      = (17.26)²
      ≈ 297.9.

   Denominator interference part:
      Σₘ β_{m,1} · (η_m · β_{m,1} + η_m · β_{m,2})
      = Σₘ β_{m,1} · η_m · (β_{m,1} + β_{m,2})
      = Σₘ β_{m,1} · 1     (since η_m (β_{m,1}+β_{m,2})=1 for each m)
      = 100 + 50 + 2 + 1
      = 153.

   SINR_1 = 297.9 / (1 + 153) ≈ 1.934.

   R_1 = log₂(1 + 1.934) ≈ log₂(2.934) ≈ 1.55 bits/s/Hz.

   By symmetry, SINR_2 = SINR_1 ≈ 1.934, R_2 ≈ 1.55.

OBSERVATION. The equal-split, full-power allocation is likely NOT the
max-min optimum (which, per Section III-B, tends to reduce power at APs
with very strong links). We'll see in the next file (III-B/C/D) that a
heuristic exponential rule p_m = e^(−α·β_max) gives better 5%-outage.

KEY TAKEAWAYS:
  1. CB is distributed: each AP only needs its own channel estimates and
     the power coefficients sent by the NC.
  2. The achievable-rate SINR depends only on large-scale fading (β_mk
     and γ_mk), not on instantaneous small-scale fading, thanks to
     channel hardening + statistical CSI at users.
  3. The per-AP constraint ties together the K power coefficients at AP m
     but keeps different APs independent.
  4. Max-min is quasi-concave, solvable by bisection with an SOCP
     feasibility test at each iteration.
  5. Optimal power control has high online complexity — motivation for
     the heuristics in the next file.
