PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

In Section III we used Conjugate Beamforming (CB), the simplest possible
precoder: each AP phase-rotates its data symbol by the conjugate of its
own channel estimate, and lets all the APs' contributions add up at the
target user. The downside of CB is that, while signals add constructively
at the intended user, they also LEAK into OTHER users and create
multi-user interference (MUI). CB does nothing about it.

Section IV studies a smarter precoder: ZERO-FORCING (ZF). The idea is
drastic: precode the data so that user j's signal DELIVERS ZERO ENERGY
TO USER k for every k ≠ j. In other words, multi-user interference is
mathematically canceled at design time.

THE MAGIC TRICK. If Ĝ is the M×K matrix of channel estimates (rows = APs,
columns = users) and if the NC pre-multiplies the data vector s by a
special matrix A_ZF = Ĝ* (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹ P, then the signal arriving at user k
is exactly √η_k · s_k (plus noise and estimation error), with NO
contribution from s_j for j ≠ k. This is because (Ĝᵀ) · (Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹) =
I_K by construction — the K×K identity matrix. Each row of this product
isolates exactly one user.

WHY DOESN'T CB DO THIS? Because CB computes A = Ĝ* (no inverse). To
actually cancel interference, you need to INVERT the K×K Gram matrix
(ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹. This is an operation that requires KNOWING ALL COLUMNS OF Ĝ
TOGETHER — you cannot do it at individual APs in parallel. The inversion
has to happen at a CENTRAL node (the NC), and then the M×K precoding
coefficients have to be sent back to the APs over the backhaul. ZF is
CENTRALIZED by nature; CB is distributed.

THE PRICE OF CANCELING INTERFERENCE. ZF uses M degrees of freedom to
"null" the K−1 other users, leaving M−K+1 effective degrees of freedom
for each user's signal. When M ≫ K, there are plenty of degrees of
freedom to spare, so ZF doesn't cost much in array gain. When M is close
to K, though, ZF becomes wasteful — it spends almost all its energy
fighting its own interference.

WHAT THIS FILE COVERS:
  (1) The ZF precoder structure (Eq. 11) and received signal (Eq. 12).
  (2) Theorem 1: an achievable-rate SINR formula for ZF that correctly
      accounts for the channel estimation error ~g_mk (Eq. 13-14).
  (3) The max-min optimization problem (17) — note it's QUASI-LINEAR
      (linear numerator and linear denominator in η), unlike CB which
      was quasi-concave (quadratic numerator, linear denominator).
  (4) Lemma 1: a surprisingly deep structural result about the ZF
      max-min problem, proved via M-matrices and Perron-Frobenius.
      It says that any feasible point achieving SINR ≥ t elementwise
      DOMINATES the point that achieves SINR = t. This monotonicity
      lets us reduce the convex feasibility check to a LINEAR SYSTEM
      OF K EQUATIONS — a massive complexity reduction.

What this file does NOT cover (that's in the next file):
  - The low-complexity closed-form Eq. (21), derived from the perfect-CSI
    Lemma 2.
  - Proposition 2, the ε-optimality theorem.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1  ZF PROTOCOL (PAGE 5, TOP OF SECTION IV)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Unlike CB, the ZF protocol requires ALL channel estimates to be collected
at the NC:

  Step 1. AP m estimates β_mk for k=1,…,K (long-term) and sends these to
          the NC.
  Step 2. Users synchronously transmit pilot sequences ψ_i.
  Step 3. AP m computes ĝ_mk and sends them to the NC over the backhaul.
  Step 4. NC computes power coefficients η_k (only K of them now, not MK).
  Step 5. NC forms the ZF precoding matrix A_ZF using Ĝ and P, then sends
          the M×K precoding coefficients BACK to the APs.

The critical difference from CB (compare with Sec. III):
  • ĝ_mk must be shipped from AP m to the NC (this is the "fronthaul").
  • A_ZF must be shipped from NC back to APs.
  • The backhaul/fronthaul traffic is M·K fast-varying complex numbers
    per coherence block, compared to M·K slow-varying real power
    coefficients in CB. ZF is MUCH heavier on the network link.

In exchange, we get total cancellation of inter-user interference in the
estimated channel, which is significantly more energy-efficient.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  BUILDING THE ZF PRECODING MATRIX (Eq. 11)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STARTING POINT: the classical ZF pseudoinverse (e.g., Tse & Viswanath
Ch. 10):

       A  =  Ĝ* · ( Ĝᵀ Ĝ* )⁻¹           (classical ZF pseudoinverse)

This matrix satisfies Ĝᵀ A = I_K by direct computation:
       Ĝᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹  =  (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*) · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹  =  I_K.

Interpretation: if we transmit x = A s, then each user k receives
(ĝᵀ_k) · A · s = eᵀ_k · s = s_k (assuming ĝ = g, perfect CSI). That's
exact interference cancellation.

POWER SCALING. We cannot transmit A s directly — it doesn't respect
per-AP power constraints. We need to scale the columns (one per user)
by some factors {√η_k, k=1,…,K}, gathered in a diagonal matrix
P := diag(√η_1, …, √η_K):

       A_ZF  =  Ĝ* · ( Ĝᵀ Ĝ* )⁻¹ · P                              (11)

       x = √(ρ_f) · A_ZF · s = √(ρ_f) · Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P · s.

WHY SHOULD η DEPEND ONLY ON k, NOT ON (m, k)?

The paper shows this via a simple necessary condition. Writing the
precoding matrix as A_ZF = A ⊙ H (Hadamard product) with [H]_mk = √η_mk
would require:

       [A_ZF]_mk  =  √η_mk · a_mk.

For Ĝᵀ A_ZF to equal a diagonal matrix (so interference across users
disappears), the condition is that the rows of Ĝᵀ A_ZF must have all
off-diagonal entries zero. A quick calculation shows this requires
η_{1,k} = η_{2,k} = ⋯ = η_{M,k} for every k — i.e., all APs must use
the same power coefficient η_k for a given user. Hence η_mk reduces to
just η_k. Only K power coefficients, not M·K.

This is a strong structural constraint: ZF TIES the per-user power
coefficient across all APs. CB allowed each AP to independently decide
how much to spend on each user; ZF does not.

One consequence: the ZF problem has K variables (η_1, …, η_K) instead
of M·K. That seems smaller. However, the per-AP constraint (16) couples
all users together through the matrix inversion, which is why Lemma 1
is needed to make this tractable.

A REMARK ON SUB-OPTIMALITY [28]. Wiesel, Eldar, Shamai (2008) showed
that under per-antenna power constraints, the "natural" ZF pseudoinverse
is generally suboptimal — the true optimal precoder subject to per-antenna
constraints is the GENERALIZED inverse solution to a different optimization
problem. The authors acknowledge this but choose the standard form (11)
"for simplicity". The gap, in practice, is small.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3  RECEIVED SIGNAL DECOMPOSITION (Eq. 12)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

User k's received signal is:

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f) · g_kᵀ · A_ZF · s  +  w_k.

Split g_k = ĝ_k + ~g_k (estimate + error) and substitute A_ZF from (11):

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f) · (ĝ_k + ~g_k)ᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹ · P · s  +  w_k.

Now, using the identity Ĝᵀ · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ = I_K, and noting that ĝ_kᵀ
is exactly the k-th ROW of Ĝᵀ, we have:

       ĝ_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹  =  eᵀ_k,    (k-th row of I_K)

       so  ĝ_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹ · P · s  =  eᵀ_k · P · s
                                            =  √η_k · s_k.

Multiplying by √(ρ_f):

       √(ρ_f) · ĝ_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹ · P · s  =  √(ρ_f η_k) · s_k.

This is the SIGNAL PART, denoted J_0:

       J_0  =  √(ρ_f η_k) · s_k.                                  (12a)

The REMAINDER comes from ~g_k (the estimation-error component):

       J_1  =  √(ρ_f) · ~g_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (Ĝᵀ Ĝ*)⁻¹ · P · s.           (12b)

Notice that J_1 contains contributions from s_1, …, s_K (all users'
symbols), because ~g_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ is NOT eᵀ_k — it's a generic
K-vector depending on the random error. So the estimation error leaks
power from OTHER users into user k.

Putting it together:

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f η_k) · s_k   +   √(ρ_f) · ~g_kᵀ · Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P s
                 ─────┬─────             ───────────────┬───────────────
                      J_0                                J_1
                                +  w_k.                                 (12)

CRITICAL OBSERVATION: J_0 is deterministic given (s_k, η_k) — there is
NO channel-hardening approximation needed for the signal part. It's an
exact statement because the ZF precoder gives a deterministic effective
channel gain √η_k. This is UNLIKE the CB case, where J_0 was (Σₘ √η_mk
γ_mk) · s_k — an expectation of |ĝ_mk|² was needed.

Why? Because ZF explicitly inverts the channel Gram matrix, producing a
deterministic diagonal effective channel, whereas CB only does a linear
pre-twist, producing a random effective channel that happens to
concentrate by channel hardening.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  THEOREM 1 — ZF ACHIEVABLE RATE (Eq. 13)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STATEMENT. An achievable rate for user k with ZF precoder is

       R_k  =  log₂( 1 + SINR_{k,ZF} ),

                      ρ_f · η_k
       SINR_{k,ZF}  =  ───────────────────────                    (13)
                      1  +  ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{ki}

where the parameter vector ν_k = (ν_{k,1}, …, ν_{k,K}) is:

       ν_k  =  diag{ 𝔼[ (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · Ĝᵀ · 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ] }
                                                                  (14)

and 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] is a diagonal matrix with (β_mk − γ_mk) on its m-th
diagonal element.

PROOF (Appendix C, reworked).

Step 1: J_0, J_1, w_k are mutually uncorrelated.
  - w_k is independent of everything (receiver noise).
  - J_0 = √(ρ_f η_k) · s_k depends only on s_k.
  - J_1 depends on ~g_k and {s_i}ᴷᵢ₌₁.
  - Since ~g_k ⊥ ĝ_k (MMSE orthogonality) and ~g_k ⊥ w_k, J_1 is
    uncorrelated with J_0 (the s_k · s*_k cross term vanishes because
    the coefficient in J_1 acting on s_k is a random variable that is
    uncorrelated with the deterministic coefficient in J_0).

Step 2: Apply worst-case uncorrelated-noise bound (Hassibi-Hochwald):

                  𝔼[|J_0|²]
       SINR_{k,ZF}  =  ─────────────────.
                  1  +  𝔼[|J_1|²]

Step 3: Compute 𝔼[|J_0|²].
       |J_0|²  =  ρ_f · η_k · |s_k|².
       𝔼[|J_0|²]  =  ρ_f · η_k · 1  =  ρ_f · η_k.                 (signal)

Step 4: Compute 𝔼[|J_1|²]. This is the hard part.

       |J_1|²  =  ρ_f · |  ~g_kᵀ · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P · s  |²

       𝔼[|J_1|²]  =  ρ_f · 𝔼[ (~g_kᵀ Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P s)
                              · (~g_kᵀ Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P s)ᴴ ].

The inner product structure allows us to write this as a trace:
       𝔼[|J_1|²]  =  ρ_f · 𝔼[ tr( P ssᴴ P · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ Ĝᵀ · ~g*_k ~g_kᵀ
                                    · Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ) ]
                  =  ρ_f · tr( P · 𝔼[ssᴴ] · P · 𝔼[(ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ Ĝᵀ ~g*_k ~g_kᵀ
                                            Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹] ).

Since 𝔼[ssᴴ] = I_K (data symbols uncorrelated, unit power) and
P · P = diag(η_1, …, η_K):

       𝔼[|J_1|²]  =  ρ_f · tr( diag(η_1,…,η_K) ·
                         𝔼[(ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ Ĝᵀ 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹] ).

The inner 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] is a matrix of SECOND moments of the estimation
error vector ~g_k = (~g_{1k}, …, ~g_{Mk})ᵀ. Since components are
independent across APs (pilot-orthogonality + independent fading), this
is a diagonal matrix with [β_mk − γ_mk] on the m-th diagonal.

Step 5: The trace of a diagonal matrix × another matrix equals the inner
product of the diagonal vectors. So:

       𝔼[|J_1|²]  =  ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{ki}                    (interference)

where ν_{ki} is the i-th diagonal entry of

       𝔼[(ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · Ĝᵀ · 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹].

This is exactly the definition (14) of ν_k. ∎

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.5  INTERPRETING ν_{ki}
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The quantity ν_{ki} represents the effective interference leaked from
user i into user k due to channel estimation error. Roughly:

       ν_{ki}  ≈  Σₘ (β_mk − γ_mk) · |[A]_mi|²

where A = Ĝ* (Ĝᵀć*)⁻¹ is the normalized ZF pseudoinverse. Think of
|[A]_mi|² as the "amount of energy AP m spends per unit η_i when
transmitting to user i", and (β_mk − γ_mk) as the "amount of
unestimated channel" between AP m and user k. The product is the
interference leakage.

If channel estimation were perfect (β_mk − γ_mk → 0), all ν_{ki} would
be zero, the denominator would be 1, and SINR_{k,ZF} would simply be
ρ_f · η_k. That's the "perfect CSI" case which motivates Lemma 2 in
Section IV-B (next file).

If estimation is poor (large estimation error), ν_{ki} grows and SINR
degrades — ZF is particularly sensitive to estimation error, because
its interference cancellation was based on the ESTIMATED channel, not
the true channel.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  PER-AP TRANSMIT POWER (Eq. 16) — DERIVATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The expected transmit power of AP m is:

       𝔼[|x_m|²]  =  ρ_f · 𝔼[ (aᵀ_[m] · a*_[m]) ]

where aᵀ_[m] is the m-th ROW of A_ZF (the column of M×K precoding
coefficients that AP m uses).

Expanding:
       aᵀ_[m]  =  (ĝᴴ_[m]) · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P,

where ĝ_[m]ᴴ is the m-th row of Ĝᴴ (equivalently, the conjugate-transpose
of the m-th column of Ĝ).

Squared norm:
       |a_[m]|²  =  (aᵀ_[m]) · (a*_[m])
                 =  (ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P)
                   · (Pᴴ · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻ᴴ · ĝ_[m])
                 =  ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P² · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻ᴴ · ĝ_[m]
                 =  tr( P² · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻ᴴ · ĝ_[m] · ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ).

Taking expectation:
       𝔼[|a_[m]|²]  =  tr( P² · 𝔼[ (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻ᴴ · ĝ_[m] · ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ] ).

Since P² = diag(η_1, …, η_K), we can write:

       𝔼[|a_[m]|²]  =  Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{mi},

where

       ν_m  =  diag{ 𝔼[ (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · ĝ_[m] · ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ] }  (15)

and ν_{mi} is its i-th diagonal entry.

So:
       𝔼[|x_m|²]  =  ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{mi},                    (16)

and the per-AP power constraint 𝔼[|x_m|²] ≤ ρ_f becomes:

       Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{mi}  ≤  1,    ∀m ∈ {1,…,M}.                 (17b)

Note that ν_m is indexed by AP m — it's an M×K list of statistics. These
are computed at the NC by averaging over small-scale fading realizations.

IMPORTANT NOTE. There are TWO DIFFERENT ν quantities in Section IV:
  • ν_k (Eq. 14) — used in the SINR denominator. Indexed by user k,
    captures interference leakage.
  • ν_m (Eq. 15) — used in the per-AP power constraint. Indexed by AP m,
    captures "power cost" per η_i.

They are defined by DIFFERENT expressions but look similar. The ν_{mi}
in constraint (17b) and the ν_{ki} in SINR (13) are related but not
the same quantity; keep them straight.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  MAX-MIN ZF POWER CONTROL (Eq. 17)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Stacking η = (η_1, …, η_K) ∈ ℝ₊ᴷ, the problem is:

       maximize_η    min_k   ρ_f · η_k / (1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{ki})  (17a)

       subject to    Σᵢ η_i · ν_{mi}  ≤  1,    ∀m ∈ {1,…,M}.          (17b)

Compare with CB's Eq. (6):
  • Only K variables now (versus M·K for CB).
  • Numerator of SINR is LINEAR in η (just η_k), not quadratic
    (Σ √η_mk γ_mk)².
  • Denominator is also linear in η.
  • Per-AP constraints are LINEAR in η (same as CB).

SO the whole problem (17) has:
  - Objective: ratio of two LINEAR functions (quasi-linear).
  - Constraints: linear.

Unlike CB (quasi-CONCAVE), the ZF problem is QUASI-LINEAR. Quasi-linear
functions are ratios of linear functions and are both quasi-concave AND
quasi-convex — a "fractional linear" function. The bisection method still
applies.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.8  BISECTION FOR (17) (Eq. 18)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The feasibility problem for a given target SINR t:

       find η
       s.t.    SINR_{k,ZF}(η) ≥ t,    k=1,…,K,                     (18a)
               Σᵢ η_i · ν_{mi} ≤ 1,    m=1,…,M.                    (18b)

The SINR constraint SINR_{k,ZF}(η) ≥ t expands to:

       ρ_f · η_k  ≥  t · (1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{ki})
       η_k  ≥  t/ρ_f + t · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{ki}
       η_k − t · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{ki}  ≥  t/ρ_f.

Collecting all K such constraints (one per user k=1,…,K):

       (I_K − t · B) · η  ≥  (t/ρ_f) · 1_K,                       (†)

where B ∈ ℝᴷ×ᴷ has entries [B]_{ki} = ν_{ki}.

But wait — the paper says in Appendix D that B has [B]_{ij} = ν_{ij} for
i ≠ j and [B]_{ii} = 0. Let me re-read. From Appendix D line:
"Define matrix B ∈ ℂᴷ×ᴷ, [B]_{ij} = ν_{ij} for i ≠ j and [B]_{ii} = 0".

So actually B is the "off-diagonal-only" version of the ν_{ki} matrix.
Let's redo: SINR_{k,ZF}(η) = t means

       ρ_f η_k / (1 + ρ_f Σᵢ η_i ν_{ki})  =  t
       ρ_f η_k  =  t + t ρ_f Σᵢ η_i ν_{ki}
       ρ_f η_k − t ρ_f Σᵢ η_i ν_{ki}  =  t
       ρ_f η_k (1 − t ν_{kk}) − t ρ_f Σᵢ≠k η_i ν_{ki}  =  t

If we define B as the off-diagonal matrix of ν_{ki}'s and incorporate the
diagonal ν_{kk} differently, and set τ = 1/t:
       (B − τ · I) · η  +  d  =  0,    (*)

where d = (1/ρ_f, …, 1/ρ_f)ᵀ  ∈ ℝᴷ (after rescaling appropriately).

Actually let me go with the paper's exact claim. The paper says (with
λ_1 = 1/t):
       (B − λ_1 I) η  +  d  =  0.

The precise algebra involves absorbing the diagonal ν_{kk} into λ_1. This
is a SYSTEM OF K LINEAR EQUATIONS in η. Solving it gives (uniquely, if
the matrix is invertible) η(t) — the UNIQUE power vector achieving
SINR_k(η) = t for all k.

The key insight of Lemma 1 (next subsection) is that this η(t) can be used
to check feasibility of the INEQUALITY problem (18) at no extra cost:
you just solve the EQUALITY system (*) and check whether the resulting η
satisfies (17b). No convex feasibility solver is needed.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.9  LEMMA 1 — MONOTONICITY (Appendix D)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STATEMENT. Suppose η and η' > 0 are two power vectors such that
SINR_{k,ZF}(η) = t and SINR_{k,ZF}(η') ≥ t for all k=1,…,K. Then

       η'  ≥  η    (element-wise).

In words: if η EQUALIZES the SINRs at level t, and η' achieves SINR ≥ t
for everyone, then η' DOMINATES η component-wise.

WHY THIS IS NON-TRIVIAL. Because (17) is a system of coupled fractional
linear constraints, it's not obvious that a "uniform-better-than-t"
vector η' must be term-wise larger. It could be that η' achieves t + ε₁
on user 1 while η achieves exactly t on user 1, yet η'_1 < η_1 (maybe
η' compensates by being bigger on other components). Lemma 1 says this
cannot happen.

PROOF SKETCH (Appendix D, reworked for clarity).

The proof uses PERRON–FROBENIUS theory for non-negative matrices +
M-MATRIX theory.

A non-negative matrix B ∈ ℝᴷ×ᴷ (i.e., with all entries ≥ 0) has a
"Perron eigenvalue" r ≥ 0 that is real, non-negative, and is the LARGEST
in absolute value among all eigenvalues. It has a corresponding non-
negative eigenvector.

An M-matrix is a matrix of the form (sI − B) where B ≥ 0 and s > r (the
Perron eigenvalue of B). M-matrices are INVERTIBLE, and their inverse is
ENTRY-WISE NON-NEGATIVE.

THE PROOF GOES LIKE THIS (paraphrased from Appendix D):

  (i)   Given that SINR_{k,ZF}(η') ≥ t for all k, and that SINR is
        continuous in η', one can CONTINUOUSLY reduce components of η'
        (whichever currently gives the max SINR) until equality
        SINR_{k,ZF}(η^(2)) = t_2 is achieved for all k, where t_2 ≥ t
        and η^(2) ≤ η'.

  (ii)  The vector η^(2) now satisfies an equality condition:
        (B − λ_2 I) η^(2) + d = 0, where λ_2 = 1/t_2.
        Similarly, the vector η satisfies (B − λ_1 I) η + d = 0, where
        λ_1 = 1/t. Here B is the "coupling matrix" (ν_{ij}-based).

  (iii) Subtract: B (η^(2) − η) − λ_2 η^(2) + λ_1 η = 0.

  (iv)  Use Perron-Frobenius: let v > 0 be the left Perron eigenvector
        of B with eigenvalue r ≥ 0. Multiplying from the left by vᵀ gives
        r · vᵀ (η^(2) − η) = λ_2 vᵀ η^(2) − λ_1 vᵀ η.
        Since vᵀ η, vᵀ η^(2) > 0 and λ_2 ≤ λ_1 (because t_2 ≥ t), some
        algebra shows r < λ_2 ≤ λ_1.

  (v)   Hence both (λ_1 I − B) and (λ_2 I − B) are M-matrices
        (invertible with non-negative inverses).

  (vi)  Therefore η = (λ_1 I − B)⁻¹ d ≥ 0, and
        η^(2) = η + (λ_2 I − B)⁻¹ · (λ_1 − λ_2) · 1... (after
        rearrangement).

  (vii) Since (λ_2 I − B)⁻¹ is non-negative and (λ_1 − λ_2) ≥ 0, we
        conclude η^(2) ≥ η element-wise. Combined with η^(2) ≤ η', we
        get η' ≥ η^(2) ≥ η. ∎

The Perron-Frobenius-based proof is elegant and illustrates the deep
structural property of ZF max-min problems: their feasibility region has
a "lattice-like" structure in which the equality solution is the unique
minimal element.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.10  COROLLARY 1 — REDUCTION OF THE FEASIBILITY CHECK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STATEMENT. If the linear system SINR_{k,ZF}(η) = t (∀k) yields a solution
η that does NOT satisfy the per-AP constraints (17b), then NO η'
satisfying SINR_{k,ZF}(η') ≥ t (∀k) satisfies (17b) either.

PROOF. By Lemma 1, any such η' ≥ η element-wise. Therefore
       Σᵢ η'_i · ν_{m₀i} ≥ Σᵢ η_i · ν_{m₀i} > 1
for the same AP m₀ where η fails the per-AP constraint (the strict
inequality because ν_{m₀i} ≥ 0 and at least one strict increase). So η'
also fails. ∎

PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: instead of solving a CONVEX FEASIBILITY problem
at each bisection step (which would be an SOCP), we solve a single SYSTEM
OF K LINEAR EQUATIONS — O(K³) complexity — and check whether the
resulting η vector satisfies M simple inequalities.

COST PER BISECTION STEP:
  • Old (CB's approach): one SOCP, cost ~ O((MK)^{3.5}) (interior point).
  • New (ZF's approach via Corollary 1): one K×K linear solve, cost O(K³).
                                          plus M inequality checks.

For M = 128, K = 16, this is O(16³) = 4096 arithmetic ops per step, versus
~O((2048)^{3.5}) ≈ 10¹² for the SOCP approach. A TRILLIONFOLD SPEEDUP.
This is one of the most important computational contributions of the
paper.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.11  HOW TO ESTIMATE ν_k AND ν_m
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Both ν_k and ν_m are expectations over small-scale fading (h_mk). They
depend ONLY on large-scale β_mk, but computing them in closed form is
hard (they involve inverses of random K×K matrices).

The paper gives TWO estimation methods:

METHOD 1: Monte Carlo dummy variables.
  Whenever the β_mk change (slowly, every few seconds), the NC generates
  MANY "dummy" complex Gaussians ĝ_{mk}^dummy ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk) (matching
  the statistics of the real estimate). For each dummy realization, it
  computes the inner expression
     (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · ĝ_[m] · ĝᴴ_[m] · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹
  and averages over many samples to estimate ν_m (and similarly ν_k).
  The optimal power allocation (17) is then re-run only when β changes,
  so this is amortized over many coherence intervals.

METHOD 2: Exponential smoothing.
  Whenever the NC receives a new real channel estimate Ĝ, it computes
  the instantaneous ν_{m,curr} from Ĝ and updates:
       ν̂_m  ←  θ · ν̂_m + (1 − θ) · ν_{m,curr}
  with some θ ∈ (0, 1). This is a recursive low-pass filter.

Both methods result in estimates of ν_k and ν_m that can be used in
(13), (14), (15), (17). Method 1 is cleaner but requires an offline
Monte Carlo batch. Method 2 is cheaper but only tracks if β changes
slowly enough.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1  ZERO-FORCING PRECODING: A BRIEF HISTORY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ZF precoding has its origins in:

  • B. R. Vojcic and W. M. Jang, "Transmitter precoding in synchronous
    multiuser communications," IEEE Trans. Commun., vol. 46, no. 10,
    pp. 1346–1355, Oct. 1998. [Early linear precoding for multi-user CDMA.]

  • M. Joham, W. Utschick, and J. A. Nossek, "Linear transmit processing
    in MIMO communications systems," IEEE Trans. Signal Process.,
    vol. 53, no. 8, pp. 2700–2712, Aug. 2005. [Comprehensive treatment
    of linear precoding including ZF and MMSE.]

  • G. Caire and S. Shamai, "On the achievable throughput of a multi-
    antenna Gaussian broadcast channel," IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory,
    vol. 49, no. 7, pp. 1691–1706, Jul. 2003. [Compared ZF to DPC.]

  • T. Yoo and A. Goldsmith, "On the optimality of multiantenna broadcast
    scheduling using zero-forcing beamforming," IEEE J. Sel. Areas
    Commun., vol. 24, no. 3, pp. 528–541, Mar. 2006. [Showed that with
    user selection, ZF achieves the same asymptotic sum-rate as DPC.]

  • D. Tse and P. Viswanath, Fundamentals of Wireless Communication,
    Cambridge Univ. Press, 2005. Ch. 10 (linear MIMO precoding).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2  PER-ANTENNA POWER CONSTRAINTS IN ZF
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The fact that the classical ZF pseudoinverse is sub-optimal under per-
antenna power constraints was formally analyzed in:

  • A. Wiesel, Y. C. Eldar, S. Shamai, "Zero-forcing precoding and
    generalized inverses," IEEE Trans. Signal Process., vol. 56, no. 9,
    pp. 4409–4418, Sep. 2008. [Reference [28] of the paper.]

Their finding: in the per-antenna power-constrained problem, the optimal
linear zero-interference precoder is NOT Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ but a more general
"generalized-inverse" matrix that depends on the power constraints
themselves. In practice, this gap is small for the cell-free setting,
so Nayebi et al. use the standard ZF for analytical tractability.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  PERRON-FROBENIUS IN POWER CONTROL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The use of Perron-Frobenius theory to analyze max-min SINR problems has
a long tradition in wireless power control. The foundational reference:

  • R. D. Yates, "A framework for uplink power control in cellular radio
    systems," IEEE J. Sel. Areas Commun., vol. 13, no. 7,
    pp. 1341–1347, Sep. 1995.

Yates's "standard interference function" framework proves that many
power-control problems converge under iterative updates because of an
underlying non-negative-matrix fixed-point structure. The M-matrix
argument used by Nayebi et al. in Appendix D is in the same spirit.

Further key references:
  • M. Schubert, H. Boche, "Solution of the multiuser downlink beamforming
    problem with individual SINR constraints," IEEE Trans. Veh. Technol.,
    vol. 53, no. 1, pp. 18–28, Jan. 2004.
  • R. A. Horn and C. R. Johnson, Matrix Analysis, Cambridge Univ. Press,
    2nd ed., 2013. Ch. 8: Perron-Frobenius theory.
  • R. J. Plemmons, "M-matrix characterizations: nonsingular M-matrices,"
    Linear Algebra Appl., vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 175–188, 1977.
    [Reference [31] of the paper.]

PART 5 — NUMERICAL EXAMPLE

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1  TOY EXAMPLE: M = 3, K = 2, PERFECT CSI
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Assume perfect channel estimation (β_mk − γ_mk = 0), so ν_{ki} = 0 for
all i, and the SINR formula collapses to:

       SINR_{k,ZF}  =  ρ_f · η_k      (no interference term)
       R_k = log₂(1 + ρ_f η_k).

Channel estimates (fictitious for illustration):

       Ĝ = [  2    0.5  ]
           [ 1.5    1   ]
           [  0.5   2   ]

ĜᵀĜ* = [  4 + 2.25 + 0.25   ,  1 + 1.5 + 1    ] = [ 6.5   3.5 ]
       [  1 + 1.5 + 1        ,  0.25 + 1 + 4  ]   [ 3.5   5.25]

det(ĜᵀĜ*) = 6.5·5.25 − 3.5² = 34.125 − 12.25 = 21.875.

(ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ = (1/21.875) · [  5.25   −3.5  ]
                         [ −3.5    6.5   ]
                       = [  0.240   −0.160 ]
                         [ −0.160    0.297 ]

A_classical = Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹:

       Row 1 (AP 1): (2, 0.5) · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹
                   = (2·0.240 + 0.5·(−0.160),  2·(−0.160) + 0.5·0.297)
                   = (0.480 − 0.080,  −0.320 + 0.149)
                   = (0.400, −0.171)

       Row 2 (AP 2): (1.5, 1) · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹
                   = (1.5·0.240 + 1·(−0.160),  1.5·(−0.160) + 1·0.297)
                   = (0.360 − 0.160,  −0.240 + 0.297)
                   = (0.200, 0.057)

       Row 3 (AP 3): (0.5, 2) · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹
                   = (0.5·0.240 + 2·(−0.160),  0.5·(−0.160) + 2·0.297)
                   = (0.120 − 0.320,  −0.080 + 0.594)
                   = (−0.200, 0.514)

Verify: Ĝᵀ · A_classical = ? Should be I_2.
   Column 1 of A_classical: (0.400, 0.200, −0.200)ᵀ.
   Ĝᵀ · column 1  =  (2·0.400 + 1.5·0.200 + 0.5·(−0.200),
                       0.5·0.400 + 1·0.200 + 2·(−0.200))
                 =  (0.800 + 0.300 − 0.100,  0.200 + 0.200 − 0.400)
                 =  (1.000, 0.000).   ✓

So the ZF precoder delivers s_1 to user 1 and nothing to user 2.

Now compute the ν_{mi}'s (per-AP power cost, Eq. 15 but
pc-version since we assumed perfect CSI).

       [A_classical]_{m,i}  =  the (m, i) entry we just computed.
       |[A_classical]_{m,i}|²  gives the "power cost for unit η_i at AP m".

   AP 1: (0.400)² = 0.160  and  (−0.171)² = 0.0292.
         ν_{1,1} ≈ 0.160,  ν_{1,2} ≈ 0.0292.
   AP 2: (0.200)² = 0.040  and  (0.057)² = 0.00325.
         ν_{2,1} ≈ 0.040,  ν_{2,2} ≈ 0.00325.
   AP 3: (−0.200)² = 0.040  and  (0.514)² = 0.264.
         ν_{3,1} ≈ 0.040,  ν_{3,2} ≈ 0.264.

(These are for a single realization; the paper's ν_m is an expectation
 over small-scale fading, but this illustrates the structure.)

Per-AP constraint (17b):
   AP 1:  0.160 · η_1 + 0.0292 · η_2 ≤ 1.
   AP 2:  0.040 · η_1 + 0.00325 · η_2 ≤ 1.
   AP 3:  0.040 · η_1 + 0.264 · η_2 ≤ 1.

Under PERFECT CSI (ν_{ki} = 0), SINR_{k,ZF} = ρ_f · η_k. Max-min says
η_1 = η_2 = η (equalize). The binding constraint is the one that first
hits 1. Let's check with η_1 = η_2 = η:
   AP 1: 0.189 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 5.29.
   AP 2: 0.0433 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 23.1.
   AP 3: 0.304 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 3.29.

The binding constraint is AP 3 (it has the largest value of ν_{3,1}+ν_{3,2}).
So η* = 3.29, and the max-min SINR is ρ_f · 3.29. For ρ_f = 1 (normalized),
that's SINR = 3.29, R = log₂(4.29) ≈ 2.10 bits/s/Hz.

OBSERVATION: AP 3 (the AP with the most "expensive" ν_{3i}) determines
the bottleneck. In the general case, Lemma 2 (next file) shows the same
thing: at least one AP is at full power.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2  PRACTICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM. In a cell-free system with M = 4 APs and K = 3 users, perfect CSI,
suppose the ν_{mi} matrix (4×3) has been computed as:

       ν = | 0.2   0.3   0.1 |   AP 1
           | 0.1   0.2   0.4 |   AP 2
           | 0.3   0.1   0.2 |   AP 3
           | 0.1   0.1   0.1 |   AP 4

Under perfect CSI and uniform power allocation η_1 = η_2 = η_3 = η,
find the largest η that satisfies all four per-AP constraints. Which AP
is binding?

SOLUTION. Sum each row of ν:
   AP 1 row sum: 0.2 + 0.3 + 0.1 = 0.6  ⟹  0.6 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 1.667
   AP 2 row sum: 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.7  ⟹  0.7 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 1.429
   AP 3 row sum: 0.3 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.6  ⟹  0.6 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 1.667
   AP 4 row sum: 0.1 + 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.3  ⟹  0.3 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 3.333

The binding AP is AP 2 (smallest η threshold). So η* = 1.429.

SINR (perfect CSI) = ρ_f · 1.429 = ρ_f · 10/7.
R = log₂(1 + ρ_f · 10/7).

For ρ_f = 10, SINR = 14.29, R ≈ log₂(15.29) ≈ 3.93 bits/s/Hz.

NOTE. This is the "perfect CSI" Lemma 2 solution, which we'll formalize
in the next file. The key insight is that under perfect CSI, the
SINRs are equal, and the bottleneck AP determines the common SINR level.

PART 6 — SECTION SUMMARY

WHAT WE COVERED IN THIS FILE:

  • ZF PROTOCOL. Centralized: channel estimates shipped to NC, which
    computes A_ZF and sends precoding coefficients back to APs.
  • ZF PRECODING MATRIX (Eq. 11): A_ZF = Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P. Power
    coefficients depend only on user index k.
  • RECEIVED SIGNAL DECOMPOSITION (Eq. 12): y_k = √(ρ_f η_k) s_k + J_1
    + w_k, with J_1 carrying only the channel-estimation-error leakage.
  • THEOREM 1 (Eq. 13): SINR_{k,ZF} = ρ_f η_k / (1 + ρ_f Σᵢ η_i ν_{ki}),
    where ν_k is defined in (14).
  • APPENDIX C: full derivation of (13) via Hassibi-Hochwald worst-case
    noise + trace manipulation.
  • PER-AP POWER (Eq. 15, 16): ν_m captures the "cost per η_i" at AP m.
  • MAX-MIN PROBLEM (Eq. 17): quasi-linear; bisection applicable.
  • LEMMA 1: monotonicity — any feasible SINR ≥ t solution dominates
    the equality solution. Proved via Perron-Frobenius + M-matrices.
  • COROLLARY 1: feasibility check reduces to solving K linear equations.
    Complexity drop from O((MK)^{3.5}) to O(K³) per bisection step.
  • Two estimation methods for ν_k, ν_m (Monte Carlo, exponential
    smoothing).

KEY TAKEAWAYS:

  1. ZF trades simplicity (CB's distributed beauty) for interference
     cancellation (ZF's centralized mathematical leverage).
  2. The ZF max-min problem has only K variables (η_1, …, η_K), far
     fewer than CB's M·K. But the feasibility check is still non-trivial
     because per-AP constraints involve a K×K matrix inverse implicitly.
  3. Lemma 1 + Corollary 1 are the algorithmic GEMS of Section IV:
     they drop the per-bisection cost from a costly SOCP to a cheap
     K×K linear solve. Without these, ZF max-min power control would
     be impractical for large cell-free systems.
  4. The proof of Lemma 1 uses non-trivial linear algebra (Perron-
     Frobenius, M-matrix inverse positivity), showcasing the depth
     of the theoretical contribution.
  5. Channel estimation error ~g_mk shows up in SINR (13) through
     ν_{ki} — if estimation is good, ν_{ki} → 0 and SINR → ρ_f η_k
     (perfect-CSI regime). This motivates the closed-form heuristic
     (21) in the next file.

WHAT'S NEXT (Section IV Part 2):
  • Perfect-CSI version of the max-min problem, Eq. (19-20).
  • Lemma 2: in the perfect-CSI case, the solution has all η_k equal
    and at least one AP at full power.
  • Closed-form heuristic power, Eq. (21).
  • Proposition 2: for sufficiently large uplink pilot power, this
    heuristic is ε-optimal.
  • Complexity analysis: the heuristic (21) is NOT the bottleneck;
    the bottleneck is computing ν_m at O(MK²).
