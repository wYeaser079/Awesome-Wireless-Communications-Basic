PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

After five theory-heavy sections, the paper finally turns to experiments.
The goal is simple: "put real numbers behind the claims". The authors
simulate a realistic urban area and run Monte Carlo trials of random
user/AP placements + fading realizations to compare:

  • Cell-free CB (with optimal, full-power, heuristic fixed-power, and
    heuristic uniform power controls)
  • Cell-free ZF (with optimal and low-complexity Eq. 21 power controls)
  • Small-cell MF (with full power and max-min power control, at two
    different transmit power levels)
  • Small-cell ZF (with max-min power control, instantaneous and
    average constraints, also at two transmit power levels)

Five experiments are conducted:

  EXPERIMENT 1 (Figures 3 & 4): Confirms the counterintuitive claim
  from Section III-B — most APs in the optimal max-min solution
  transmit at LESS than full power. Shows scatter plots of p_m^opt
  vs β_m^max for CB and ZF precoders, at M ∈ {8, 32, 128} and K = 4.

  EXPERIMENT 2 (Figures 5 & 6): The central comparison of CELL-FREE
  VS SMALL-CELL. Plots the empirical CDF of per-user rates and of
  minimum rates, for M=128, K=16. The "money numbers" of the paper:
     Cell-Free CB optimal:   5.36× over small-cell MF full power
     Cell-Free ZF optimal:  10.02× over small-cell MF full power

  EXPERIMENT 3 (Figure 7): Tests whether small-cell systems can
  "catch up" by shrinking M and pumping the per-AP power up. Answer:
  No. Going from M=256 to 128 to 64 (with per-AP power scaling up by
  ×4, ×8 respectively) DEGRADES small-cell 5%-outage rate, because
  small-cell benefits from "super-diversity" when M is large.

  EXPERIMENT 4 (Figure 8): Studies the EFFECTIVE NUMBER OF APS that
  actually deliver power to a user, defined as the minimum set of APs
  providing 95% of the user's received power. Only a FRACTION of the
  M APs matter for any given user. This motivates the "scalable"
  extensions developed in later papers (e.g., Björnson-Sanguinetti 2020).

  EXPERIMENT 5 (Table I): Scales M and K together at constant ratio
  M/K = 8 (so M ∈ {64, 128, 256}, K ∈ {8, 16, 32}). Shows that cell-free
  rates GROW with network size, while small-cell rates stay flat.
  Cell-free is "scalable" in that sense; small-cell hits a ceiling.

FINAL OBSERVATIONS:
  • The CDF curves for cell-free systems are NEARLY VERTICAL — meaning
    performance is consistent across channel realizations. This is the
    "uniformly good service" property that cell-free was designed for.
  • Small-cell systems have much more variable CDFs — some users get
    great rates, others get terrible ones.
  • The low-complexity ZF allocation (Eq. 21) gives rates INDISTINGUISHABLE
    from the exact optimum (Eq. 17) — validating Proposition 2.

Section VII wraps up with the authors' summary of contributions.

2.1  SIMULATION SETUP — EVERY PARAMETER EXPLAINED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The authors carefully specify their simulation environment. Let's go
through each parameter.

(a) AREA: 2 km × 2 km SQUARE, WRAPPED AROUND.
    - Wrapping means the left edge is identified with the right edge
      and the top with the bottom, topologically forming a torus.
    - Purpose: to eliminate boundary effects. In an unwrapped area,
      users near the edge have fewer APs nearby and would skew the
      statistics; wrapping makes all points equivalent.

(b) PLACEMENT: M APs and K users independently and uniformly random.
    - Each trial, re-generate new positions. Monte Carlo averaging
      provides empirical CDFs.

(c) PATH LOSS MODEL: COST-Hata
    10·log₁₀(β_mk)  =  −136  −  35·log₁₀(d_mk)  +  X_mk,
    where d_mk is distance in km, X_mk ~ 𝒩(0, σ²_shad) is log-normal
    shadow fading with σ_shad = 8 dB.

    BREAKDOWN OF THE FORMULA:
       −136 dB:   reference path-loss at d_mk = 1 km.
       −35 dB/decade: path-loss exponent (typical urban).
       X_mk:       log-normal shadowing representing blocked/unblocked
                    NLOS scenarios.

    At d_mk = 10 m = 0.01 km:
       10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·(−2) = −136 + 70 = −66 dB (without shadow).
       β_mk ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻⁷.

    At d_mk = 100 m = 0.1 km:
       10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·(−1) = −101 dB.
       β_mk ≈ 7.9 × 10⁻¹¹.

    At d_mk = 1 km:
       10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 dB.
       β_mk ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴.

    So the dynamic range of β across the area is about 70 dB, spanning
    7 orders of magnitude. This massive dynamic range is what creates
    the "near-far" problem the max-min allocation is designed to
    combat.

(d) NOISE VARIANCE: σ²_w  =  κ · T · B · NF, where
       κ = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann constant)
       T = 290 K (room temperature)
       B = 20 MHz (bandwidth)
       NF = 10^(9/10) ≈ 7.94 (noise figure of 9 dB)

    σ²_w  =  1.38·10⁻²³ · 290 · 20·10⁶ · 7.94
          ≈  6.35 · 10⁻¹⁴ W
          ≈  −131.97 dBm.

    This is the thermal noise floor at the receiver — the standard
    baseline for wireless system design.

(e) TRANSMIT POWER: ρ_f = ρ_r = 200 mW = +23 dBm (both downlink AP power
    and uplink user power).

    Normalized uplink power ρ_r (after dividing by σ²_w):
       ρ̃_r = 0.2 W / 6.35·10⁻¹⁴ W ≈ 3.15·10¹² ≈ +125 dB.

    This is an enormously large normalized number — it means the system
    is essentially NOT noise-limited for any reasonably close AP. The
    main performance driver is interference.

(f) PILOT LENGTH: τ = K (minimum number for orthogonal pilots).

(g) CARRIER FREQUENCY: ~1.9 GHz (implied by Section II-B), though not
    explicitly stated in Section VI.

COMMENT. These parameters match a realistic urban dense-deployment
scenario consistent with 3GPP Release 16-17 New Radio (NR) assumptions.
The results should be interpretable as representative of what a real
deployment might achieve.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  EXPERIMENT 1 — SCATTER PLOTS OF p_opt VS β_max (Figures 3, 4)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This experiment validates the counterintuitive claim from Section III-B
that MOST APS IN THE OPTIMAL SOLUTION TRANSMIT AT LESS THAN FULL POWER,
and further that the optimal transmit power DECREASES with β_m^max.

SETUP:
  - K = 4 users (small to make visualization clean).
  - M ∈ {8, 32, 128} (three values).
  - Run optimization (6) for CB and (17) for ZF, over 100 channel
    realizations each.
  - For each AP m, plot (β_m^max, p_m^opt/ρ_f) as a scatter point.
  - β_m^max := max_{k=1,…,4} β_mk is the strongest β associated with AP m.

RESULTS (FIGURE 3 — CB PRECODER):

  • M = 8 (blue): The scatter is clustered near p_m^opt/ρ_f ≈ 1 for all
    values of β_m^max. Interpretation: with only 8 APs, the system is
    power-starved. Every AP should transmit at full power to maximize
    the coherent sum.

  • M = 32 (green): Pattern starts emerging. APs with small β_m^max
    stay at full power; those with larger β_m^max drop below.

  • M = 128 (red): The scatter is visibly exponential. APs with β_m^max
    > 4·10⁻¹⁰ transmit at essentially 0% of full power; APs with β_m^max
    < 1·10⁻¹⁰ transmit at near-full power. The transition is smooth
    and roughly exponential:
        p_m^opt/ρ_f  ≈  exp(−α · β_m^max),  with α fit empirically.

INTERPRETATION. In the Massive MIMO regime (M ≫ K), an AP that sits
close to a user is "over-resourced" — it doesn't need much power to
serve its user well because the direct link is strong. Transmitting at
full power would create large interference at other users. The optimal
strategy is to LET THIS AP BACK OFF so that the total interference
drops and all users benefit.

RESULTS (FIGURE 4 — ZF PRECODER): The same qualitative pattern holds,
but the scatter is tighter (less variance) because ZF's interference
cancellation means the structure is more deterministic. Again, at M=128,
most nearby APs transmit at less than full power.

ENGINEERING IMPLICATION. In real cell-free deployments with hundreds of
APs, you should NOT blindly pump power through every PA. Turn down the
amplifiers of "rich" APs (those with nearby users). This is both an
energy-efficiency and an interference-suppression argument.

The paper's heuristic power controls (III-C, III-D) are direct
descendants of this observation.

2.3  EXPERIMENT 2 — CDF COMPARISONS (Figures 5, 6)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is THE experiment — the central comparison of cell-free vs.
small-cell under max-min power control.

SETUP:
  - M = 128 APs, K = 16 users.
  - 13 curves plotted in Figure 5:
    1. Cell-Free ZF Low-Complexity Power Allocation (Eq. 21)
    2. Cell-Free ZF Optimal Power Allocation (Eq. 17)
    3. Cell-Free CB Optimal Power Allocation (Eq. 6)
    4. Cell-Free CB Heuristic Fixed-Power (Eq. 9)
    5. Cell-Free CB Heuristic Uniform Powers (Eq. 10)
    6. Cell-Free CB Full Power Transmission (Eq. 8)
    7. Small-Cell MF Full Power, Per-AP = 8ρ_f (i.e., 1.6 W)
    8. Small-Cell MF Full Power, Per-AP = ρ_f (200 mW)
    9. Small-Cell MF Max-Min, Per-AP = 8ρ_f
   10. Small-Cell MF Max-Min, Per-AP = ρ_f
   11. Small-Cell ZF Max-Min (Instant), Per-AP = 8ρ_f
   12. Small-Cell ZF Max-Min (Avg), Per-AP = 8ρ_f
   13. Small-Cell ZF Max-Min (Instant), Per-AP = ρ_f
   14. Small-Cell ZF Max-Min (Avg), Per-AP = ρ_f

  The "8ρ_f" scenario gives small-cell APs 1.6 W per antenna. Why 8?
  Because 128 APs × 200 mW = 25.6 W total = 16 APs × 1.6 W. Same total
  power, but with only K = 16 active antennas each having a larger PA.
  This is the "fair total-power comparison".

  The "ρ_f" scenario keeps each small-cell AP at 200 mW, matching the
  per-antenna hardware of cell-free. Same per-AP hardware, but lower
  total power for small-cell.

RESULTS (extracted from Figure 5 and paper's text):

At the 5%-outage level:

  Small-Cell MF Full Power (per-AP=ρ_f):  ≈  0.51 bits/s/Hz  [baseline]
  Cell-Free CB Optimal:                    ≈  2.82 bits/s/Hz
  Cell-Free ZF Optimal:                    ≈  5.28 bits/s/Hz
  Cell-Free ZF Low-Complexity (Eq. 21):   ≈  5.28 bits/s/Hz (indistinguishable)
  Cell-Free CB Heuristic Fixed-Power:     ≈  slightly below optimal
  Cell-Free CB Heuristic Uniform:         ≈  similar to fixed-power
  Cell-Free CB Full Power:                ≈  ~1.2 bits/s/Hz (half of optimal)

THE RATIO COMPUTATION:

  Cell-Free CB Optimal / Small-Cell MF Full Power
     =  2.82 / 0.51  ≈  5.36   ← "5.36-fold improvement"

  Cell-Free ZF Optimal / Small-Cell MF Full Power
     =  5.28 / 0.51  ≈  10.02  ← "10.02-fold improvement"

In the per-AP = 8ρ_f scenario (equal total power):
  CF CB / SC MF = 5.26
  CF ZF / SC MF = 9.83

So even at equal total power, cell-free still wins by nearly 10×.

FOUR KEY OBSERVATIONS FROM THE AUTHORS:

  1. Cell-Free ZF with Eq. 21 closed-form power is INDISTINGUISHABLE
     from the exact optimum. Proposition 2 is empirically validated.

  2. Cell-Free ZF > Cell-Free CB > all small-cell variants.
     The ordering confirms ZF's superiority for cell-free.

  3. Small-Cell MF FULL POWER outperforms Small-Cell MF MAX-MIN at the
     5%-outage metric (!!!). This is counterintuitive — max-min should
     improve the worst-case user. The explanation: max-min is tuned to
     improve the MINIMUM rate, which is even worse than the 5%-outage
     value. When you push up the minimum, you sometimes drag down the
     values near the 5%-outage percentile. Max-min does not promise to
     optimize the 5%-outage percentile — only the minimum. Hence at the
     5%-outage metric, max-min can underperform full power.

  4. Small-Cell ZF with INSTANTANEOUS constraint > with AVERAGE
     constraint. As discussed in the Section V analysis, this is
     because average constraint allows rare peak-power violations that
     hurt the worst-case rate.

FIGURE 6 (minimum rate CDF). Same comparisons but now plotting
min_k R_k instead of per-user R_k. Under max-min, all users get the
SAME rate, so the CDF of min_k equals the CDF of individual R_k — the
curves coincide for max-min schemes. For non-max-min schemes (e.g.,
full power), min_k is strictly less than median R_k, so the CDFs are
shifted left.

The headline observation: cell-free MAX-MIN schemes are nearly VERTICAL
CDFs, meaning min rate hardly varies with channel realization —
"uniformly good service". Small-cell CDFs are shallow, meaning the worst
user's rate depends heavily on random geometry.

2.4  EXPERIMENT 3 — DENSIFYING SMALL CELLS (Figure 7)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Question: Can small-cell networks be made competitive by REDUCING M
(fewer APs) and INCREASING per-AP power (bigger PAs)?

Intuition: maybe if small cells use all available power through fewer,
more powerful APs, they can catch up to cell-free.

SETUP:
  - K = 32 users fixed.
  - Cell-free benchmark: M = 256, per-AP = ρ_f (200 mW).
  - Small-cell variants:
    * M = 256, per-AP = ρ_f (same total power as baseline).
    * M = 128, per-AP = 4ρ_f (800 mW — bigger PAs).
    * M =  64, per-AP = 8ρ_f (1.6 W — much bigger PAs).

All three small-cell variants have APPROXIMATELY the same total
transmit power (M × per-AP-power ≈ constant), differing only in how it
is distributed.

RESULTS:

  • Cell-Free CB Optimal, M=256, 200mW:   best 5%-outage (~5 bits/s/Hz).
  • Small-Cell MF, M=256, 200mW:         modest 5%-outage.
  • Small-Cell MF, M=128, 800mW:          worse.
  • Small-Cell MF, M= 64, 1600mW:        still worse.

SO reducing M DEGRADES small-cell 5%-outage rate, EVEN AS per-AP power
grows. Why?

KEY INSIGHT — SUPER-DIVERSITY. In small-cell, each user picks the best
of M large-scale fading coefficients. Under log-normal shadowing, the
MAX of M log-normal variables has a HEAVY TAIL — the probability of
the user having at least one very-close AP grows quickly with M. The
paper calls this "super-diversity gain".

Mathematically, if β_mk are i.i.d. log-normal (which they're not
exactly, but roughly after removing distance effects), then

       E[max_{m=1,…,M} log β_mk]  ≈  log(M) · σ (in the large-M tail),

so the best-AP's β_mk grows roughly as M^σ. Doubling M substantially
improves the average user's strongest link.

When M shrinks (to 64), this super-diversity is lost. The best-AP
β_mk is much smaller, and no amount of per-AP PA amplification can
compensate — the receiver is power-saturated but still sees a bad
effective channel.

ENGINEERING IMPLICATION. For small cells to work well, you NEED a lot
of them — at least as many as cell-free. But then why bother with
small cells at all? Just use cell-free and jointly serve all users.
This experiment is a decisive argument against the "fewer bigger
small cells" design philosophy.

2.5  EXPERIMENT 4 — EFFECTIVE NUMBER OF SERVING APS (Figure 8)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Question: In a cell-free system with M = 128 APs, how many APs
ACTUALLY contribute meaningfully to each user's received signal?

DEFINITION OF "EFFECTIVE NUMBER":
  For each user k and each channel realization, compute the expected
  received power from each AP m: E[|g_mk · x_m|²] · η_m·... (some
  measure of contribution). Sort in descending order. The EFFECTIVE
  NUMBER is the minimum number of top-contributing APs whose combined
  power is 95% of the total.

SETUP: M = 128, K = 16, optimal max-min power allocation, both CB and ZF.

RESULTS (from Figure 8, approximate values):

  For CB:
    Median effective number of APs:  ~20.
    95th percentile:                  ~40.

  For ZF:
    Median effective number of APs:  ~30.
    95th percentile:                  ~55.

So for CB, the average user is effectively served by ~20 APs out of 128;
for ZF it's ~30. The remaining ~100 APs contribute very little to any
given user.

INTERPRETATION. This has HUGE practical implications:
  • You don't really need each AP to serve each user. If each AP served
    only its "effective set" of ~30 users (instead of all 16), the
    system would behave nearly identically but with vastly reduced
    backhaul, processing, and scheduling overhead.
  • This is the KEY INSIGHT behind SCALABLE cell-free massive MIMO
    (Björnson-Sanguinetti 2020): define "USER-CENTRIC CLUSTERS" where
    each user is served by a small set of nearby APs, and each AP
    serves a small set of nearby users. The net behavior is nearly
    identical to the fully-connected cell-free case, but the complexity
    is independent of total M.

Experiment 4 is thus the "motivation" experiment for the entire scalable
cell-free line of research that followed this paper (2018-2024).

WHY DOES ZF USE MORE APS THAN CB?
Intuition: ZF must cancel interference, which requires precise
knowledge of the K-dimensional interference subspace. This requires
involvement from APs that have measurable coupling to all K users,
which extends beyond the immediate neighborhood. CB, by contrast, only
needs APs near the intended user, so it "uses" fewer APs.

2.6  EXPERIMENT 5 — SCALING WITH (M, K) (Table I)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Question: How do rates scale as we grow M and K together (maintaining
M/K = 8)?

SETUP: (M, K) ∈ {(64, 8), (128, 16), (256, 32)}. Ratio M/K = 8 constant.

RESULTS (Table I, bits/s/Hz):

┌─────────────┬──────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────┐
│             │   Small-Cell MF          │   Cell-Free CB           │   Cell-Free ZF           │
│             │     Full Power           │    Optimal Power         │    Optimal Power         │
│             │ M=64  M=128  M=256       │ M=64  M=128  M=256       │ M=64  M=128  M=256       │
│             │ K=8   K=16   K=32        │ K=8   K=16   K=32        │ K=8   K=16   K=32        │
├─────────────┼──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┤
│ 5%-outage   │ 0.50  0.51   0.51        │ 2.38  2.82   3.05        │ 3.16  5.28   7.17        │
│ Median      │ 3.02  3.47   3.61        │ 2.56  2.91   3.07        │ 4.23  6.00   7.74        │
└─────────────┴──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┘

OBSERVATIONS:

(i) SMALL-CELL 5%-OUTAGE IS FLAT. 0.50, 0.51, 0.51 — essentially constant
    as (M, K) scales. Growing the network does NOT help the worst-case
    user in small-cell. This is because with M/K = 8, each user still
    has only 1 AP serving it, and that AP's β_mk is determined by the
    spatial density (~ constant in the limit). Super-diversity helps the
    median but not the tail.

(ii) CELL-FREE CB GROWS MODESTLY. 2.38 → 2.82 → 3.05. Each doubling of
     (M, K) adds ~0.3-0.5 bits/s/Hz at 5%-outage. The growth is
     sub-linear because CB does not scale its interference cancellation
     with K — more users means more MUI.

(iii) CELL-FREE ZF GROWS STRONGLY. 3.16 → 5.28 → 7.17. Each doubling
      adds ~2 bits/s/Hz at 5%-outage. The growth is closer to LINEAR
      because ZF explicitly cancels MUI, so adding more users (with
      proportionally more APs) maintains the effective array gain.

(iv) SMALL-CELL MEDIAN IS QUITE COMPETITIVE. 3.02 at (64,8) is better
     than Cell-Free CB at the same size (2.56). The reason is super-
     diversity: small-cell picks the best of M log-normal variables,
     and at the median, this beats CB's "democratic" distribution. But
     at 5%-outage, small-cell loses decisively.

RATIO FOR M=256, K=32:
     Cell-Free ZF / Small-Cell MF  =  7.17 / 0.51  ≈  14.1

So at the biggest tested size, the advantage of cell-free ZF grows from
~10× to ~14×. Cell-free scales better.

PREDICTION FROM THE TREND. If this scaling continues, then at
(M=1024, K=128), cell-free ZF might deliver 10+ bits/s/Hz at 5%-outage,
while small-cell stays around 0.5 bits/s/Hz. A 20× gap or more.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  CLOSING OBSERVATION — VERTICAL CDFs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The authors end Section VI with an aesthetic-but-deep observation: the
CDFs of cell-free systems under optimal max-min power allocation are
NEARLY VERTICAL LINES. This means:

       P( R_user < R* − ε ) ≈ 0,
       P( R_user < R* + ε ) ≈ 1,

for a small ε. In other words, with very high probability, every user
gets almost EXACTLY the same rate R* — determined by the channel
realization (large-scale fading geometry) but not by small-scale fading.

This is the "uniformly good service" property that Massive MIMO and
cell-free were designed to deliver. Contrast with traditional cellular,
where the CDF has a wide spread: some users get 100 Mbps, others get
1 Mbps, depending on where they stand. Cell-free removes this inequity.

From a product/UX standpoint, this is the most important result: it
doesn't just mean "higher rates on average" — it means "predictable
service". A mobile operator can PROMISE a minimum rate with confidence.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.8  SECTION VII — CONCLUSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper's conclusion is a compact restatement of contributions. Key
points:

  • Downlink performance of cell-free Massive MIMO analyzed in terms of
    minimum rate and 5%-outage rate.
  • Power optimization algorithms (max-min) developed for both cell-free
    and small-cell systems, with both CB and ZF precoders.
  • For cell-free CB: three low-complexity heuristic power allocations
    proposed, with only moderate loss in 5%-outage but much smaller
    complexity.
  • For cell-free ZF: optimal max-min via bisection + linear-equation
    feasibility (Lemma 1), AND a low-complexity closed-form solution
    (Eq. 21) based on perfect-CSI analysis, proven near-optimal at high
    uplink pilot power (Proposition 2), with virtually optimal
    performance in simulations.
  • Small-cell comparison baselines: MF and ZF with power control.
  • EVEN THOUGH small-cell schemes benefit from users having CSI and
    update power 40× more often, they still lose decisively at
    5%-outage and min rate.

The key numeric claim:
   Cell-free massive MIMO with ZF precoder outperforms small-cell
   systems by ABOUT 10× in 5%-outage rate.

3.1  NUMERICAL BENCHMARKS IN CELL-FREE LITERATURE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The 5%-outage metric became a de-facto standard in cell-free literature
because of its fairness interpretation. After this paper, most cell-free
papers report 5%-outage rates for their proposed schemes. Key follow-ups:

  • Björnson, Sanguinetti, "Making Cell-Free Massive MIMO Competitive
    With MMSE Processing and Centralized Implementation," IEEE TWC,
    vol. 19, no. 1, pp. 77-90, Jan. 2020. [Shows that MMSE-based
    combining (not just MF or ZF) closes much of the gap vs. cellular
    systems.]

  • Björnson, Sanguinetti, "Scalable Cell-Free Massive MIMO Systems,"
    IEEE Trans. Commun., vol. 68, no. 7, pp. 4247-4261, Jul. 2020.
    [User-centric clustering, inspired directly by this paper's
    Experiment 4 observation that only ~20 APs serve each user.]

  • Interdonato et al., "Ubiquitous cell-free massive MIMO
    communications," EURASIP J. Wireless Commun. Netw., 2019:197.
    [Comprehensive review of cell-free deployment scenarios.]

  • Demir, Björnson, Sanguinetti, "Foundations of User-Centric Cell-Free
    Massive MIMO," Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing, 2021.
    [Book-length treatment that incorporates lessons from this paper.]

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2  WHY THE VERTICAL CDF MATTERS FOR 5G/6G
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The "uniformly good service" property aligns with 3GPP's "URLLC"
(Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communications) and 6G's "eMBB++"
requirements:

  • URLLC: 99.999% reliability with bounded latency. Impossible if a
    user's rate can drop unpredictably — requires vertical CDFs.

  • XR/AR streaming in 6G: requires sustained 1 Gbps+ with low
    variance. Cell-free ZF is one of the few architectures that can
    deliver this.

  • Autonomous driving, factory automation: need predictable minimum
    rates. Cell-free ZF's CDF verticality maps directly to "guaranteed
    minimum throughput".

The paper's Experiment 5 scaling with (M, K) also supports the thesis
that cell-free is the future: as densification grows, cell-free keeps
delivering, while cellular hits a ceiling.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  THE EMERGING "SCALABILITY" CONCERN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

After this paper, a new critique emerged: while cell-free ZF IS
performance-optimal, the backhaul and computational costs scale as
O(M·K) per coherence block. For M = 1024, K = 128, this is
O(130,000) channel estimates + a 128×128 matrix inversion per block.
Is this really feasible?

The answer (again, via Björnson-Sanguinetti 2020) is the USER-CENTRIC
approach: limit each user to being served by a FIXED number of APs
(e.g., the top 30 by β value), and let each AP serve a fixed number
of users (e.g., top 20). This keeps complexity O(1) per user-AP
relationship, and Experiment 4 of THIS paper is the empirical
justification for why it works: only a fraction of APs really matter
anyway.

So the present paper not only established cell-free Massive MIMO as a
competitive architecture — it planted the seeds of the scalable
variants that followed.

PART 5 — NUMERICAL EXAMPLES

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1  EXAMPLE: ABSOLUTE THROUGHPUT AT 20 MHz BANDWIDTH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Converting spectral efficiency (bits/s/Hz) to absolute throughput
(bits/s) by multiplying with the bandwidth B = 20 MHz:

  Small-Cell MF Full Power, 5%-outage:
     0.51 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz = 10.2 Mbps
     (what the worst 5% of users experience in the small-cell system)

  Cell-Free CB Optimal, 5%-outage:
     2.82 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz = 56.4 Mbps

  Cell-Free ZF Optimal, 5%-outage:
     5.28 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz = 105.6 Mbps

So the cell-free ZF system delivers over 100 Mbps to EVERY USER (at the
5%-outage level), compared to 10 Mbps in the small-cell case. That's
the difference between "barely functional streaming video" and "1080p
video call for everyone".

At larger scales (Experiment 5, M=256, K=32):
  Cell-Free ZF 5%-outage: 7.17 · 20 = 143.4 Mbps per user.
  Cell-Free ZF median:    7.74 · 20 = 154.8 Mbps per user.

Still delivering >100 Mbps to the worst 5%. This is the performance
envelope that 6G seeks.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2  EXAMPLE: COMPUTING α IN THE EXPERIMENT 1 EXPONENTIAL FIT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Figure 3 shows p_m^opt/ρ_f vs β_m^max for M=128 CB. Eyeballing the
scatter plot (β_m^max ranges 0 to 8·10⁻¹⁰):

  At β_m^max = 1·10⁻¹⁰:  p/ρ_f ≈ 0.90.
  At β_m^max = 3·10⁻¹⁰:  p/ρ_f ≈ 0.50.
  At β_m^max = 5·10⁻¹⁰:  p/ρ_f ≈ 0.20.
  At β_m^max = 7·10⁻¹⁰:  p/ρ_f ≈ 0.08.

Fit p/ρ_f = exp(−α · β_max):
     ln(0.50) = −α · 3·10⁻¹⁰  ⟹  α ≈ 2.31 · 10⁹.
     ln(0.20) = −α · 5·10⁻¹⁰  ⟹  α ≈ 3.22 · 10⁹.
     ln(0.08) = −α · 7·10⁻¹⁰  ⟹  α ≈ 3.61 · 10⁹.

The exponent α ≈ 2.5-3.6 × 10⁹ (dependent on the exact curve fit).
The paper's offline procedure would minimize a least-squares loss over
all scatter points to get a consistent single value, say
α ≈ 3 · 10⁹.

With this α, the heuristic uniform power rule (III-D) becomes:

     η_m = exp(−3·10⁹ · β_m^max) / Σᵢ γ_mi.

A close AP (β_m^max = 1·10⁻¹⁰) gets p_m ≈ e^{−0.3} ≈ 0.74.
A medium AP (β_m^max = 4·10⁻¹⁰) gets p_m ≈ e^{−1.2} ≈ 0.30.
A far AP (β_m^max = 8·10⁻¹⁰) gets p_m ≈ e^{−2.4} ≈ 0.09.

(Note: β_m^max is "max over users", so a 7·10⁻¹⁰ value corresponds to a
SINGLE nearby user — AP is busy serving that one user and avoiding
interfering with others. The AP is CLOSE to someone but the p_m value
is SMALL. That's the "close-AP back-off" behavior.)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.3  PRACTICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM. In a cell-free deployment:
  - M = 128 APs, K = 16 users.
  - Bandwidth B = 20 MHz.
  - Cell-free ZF achieves R_{5%} = 5.28 bits/s/Hz.

(a) Compute the total system throughput (sum of all user rates) at the
    median operating point (~6 bits/s/Hz each).

(b) Compute the total system throughput at the 5%-outage level.

(c) A competing small-cell system with M = 128, K = 16, per-AP = 200 mW
    achieves R_{5%} = 0.51 bits/s/Hz. What is its total 5%-outage
    throughput?

(d) To match the cell-free ZF 5%-outage throughput, how much bandwidth
    would small-cell need?

SOLUTION.

(a) Total median throughput  =  16 users × 6 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz
                              =  1920 Mbps  ≈  1.92 Gbps.

(b) Total 5%-outage throughput (cell-free ZF) = 16 × 5.28 × 20
                                               =  1689.6 Mbps ≈ 1.69 Gbps.

(c) Total 5%-outage throughput (small-cell MF) = 16 × 0.51 × 20
                                                 =  163.2 Mbps.

(d) Small-cell would need bandwidth B' such that:
      16 × 0.51 × B' = 16 × 5.28 × 20
      B' = (5.28 / 0.51) × 20 ≈ 10.35 × 20 ≈ 207 MHz.

So to deliver the same 5%-outage throughput as cell-free ZF at 20 MHz,
the small-cell system would need ~207 MHz of spectrum — more than 10×
more. Spectrum is expensive (multi-billion-dollar auctions), so cell-free
ZF is effectively a 10× spectrum-efficiency improvement.

This is the ECONOMIC case for cell-free: it saves spectrum, which is
by far the most expensive resource in mobile networks.

WHAT WE COVERED IN THIS FILE:

  • Simulation setup (2×2 km² torus, COST-Hata path loss, 8 dB shadowing,
    20 MHz bandwidth, 9 dB noise figure, 200 mW transmit power).
  • Experiment 1: scatter plots of p_opt/ρ_f vs β_m^max for CB and ZF,
    confirming the exponential relationship for large M.
  • Experiment 2: headline CDF comparisons, showing Cell-Free ZF Optimal
    ≈ 5.28 bits/s/Hz vs. Small-Cell MF Full Power ≈ 0.51 — a 10.02×
    improvement in 5%-outage rate at M=128, K=16.
  • Experiment 3: reducing M in small-cell degrades performance despite
    higher per-AP power — super-diversity matters more than peak PA
    power.
  • Experiment 4: effective number of serving APs is only ~20-30 out of
    128, motivating user-centric clustering for future scalable cell-free.
  • Experiment 5: scaling with (M, K) at ratio 8 — small-cell 5%-outage
    is FLAT, cell-free grows. At (M=256, K=32), cell-free ZF is 14×
    better than small-cell MF.
  • Cell-free CDFs are nearly VERTICAL → uniformly good service → ideal
    for URLLC/eMBB guarantees.
  • Section VII conclusion: cell-free massive MIMO (especially with ZF)
    delivers ~10× 5%-outage improvement over small-cell systems, with
    low-complexity power allocations that are near-optimal in practice.

KEY TAKEAWAYS FROM THE NUMERICAL EVIDENCE:

  1. CELL-FREE WINS. Not by a small margin — by a factor of 10 on the
     most important metric (5%-outage). This is a decisive empirical
     result.

  2. ZF > CB. The interference-canceling benefit of ZF roughly doubles
     the 5%-outage rate compared to CB (5.28 vs 2.82). This justifies
     the extra backhaul cost of centralized ZF processing.

  3. HEURISTICS ARE NEAR-OPTIMAL. Cell-Free ZF Eq. (21) matches the
     true optimum visually. Cell-Free CB heuristic fixed/uniform are
     close to CB optimum. So low-complexity algorithms are deployable
     without performance loss.

  4. SMALL CELLS HIT A CEILING. No matter how much you densify or
     boost power, small cells cannot match cell-free at 5%-outage
     because they can only use K out of M APs.

  5. THE SCALABILITY INSIGHT. Experiment 4 shows that only a fraction
     of APs really serve each user — planting the seed for scalable
     cell-free architectures developed in subsequent literature.

  6. SPECTRUM EQUIVALENT. To match cell-free ZF's 5%-outage throughput,
     a small-cell system would need ~10× more bandwidth — a major
     economic argument for cell-free deployment.

  THE PAPER ACHIEVED FOUR THINGS:
    (i)   Derived rigorous capacity lower bounds for cell-free systems

          bisection (Eqs. 6, 17, 25, 28, 29), and proved structural

  addressed deployability. It is the DEFINITIVE treatment of
  precoding-specific max-min power control for cell-free Massive MIMO
  under both CB and ZF, and its low-complexity algorithms are still
  widely cited in the field.
