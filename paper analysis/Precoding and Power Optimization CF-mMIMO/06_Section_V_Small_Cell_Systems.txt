PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Up to Section IV, the paper has been making the case for cell-free Massive
MIMO (every user served by all APs). But is that really better than the
obvious alternative — a traditional "small cell" network where each user
is served by only ONE nearby AP? Section V builds that comparison
baseline, then uses it in Section VI to quantify the cell-free advantage.

THE SMALL-CELL SETUP. Picture the same 2 km × 2 km urban area with M = 128
APs scattered around and K = 16 users. Instead of letting all 128 APs
serve all 16 users simultaneously, you pick ONE AP to "own" each user —
typically the closest one (largest β_{m,k}). That gives you K = 16
active APs; the remaining M − K = 112 APs stay silent. Each active AP
transmits only its one user's data symbol, no precoding, no coordination.

This is the traditional cellular paradigm: a user belongs to one base
station, and that base station beams its signal directly. Small cells are
simpler than cell-free (no backhaul coordination, no central brain), and
they're what 4G/5G picocells/femtocells effectively are.

Section V analyzes this small-cell system under TWO receiver/precoder
choices:

  (V.1) MATCHED FILTER (MF) AT THE USER. Each user receives its one AP's
        signal plus leakage from other active APs. The user does
        "conjugate beamforming" at the receiver: multiply by ĝ*_{m_k,k}
        to phase-align. Because the user is served by only ONE antenna,
        there is NO channel hardening, and the achievable rate must be
        computed as an EXPECTATION over the small-scale fading.
        The result involves the EXPONENTIAL INTEGRAL function Ei(·)
        — a classical special function used in MF/MRC averaging.

  (V.2) ZERO-FORCING (ZF) ACROSS THE K SELECTED APS. If you let the K
        active APs share their channel estimates over a backhaul, they
        can JOINTLY precode using a ZF pseudo-inverse to cancel
        inter-user interference at the K selected APs. This is a
        "mini cell-free" system restricted to K APs. Theorem 2 gives
        its achievable rate.

THE PUNCH LINE (proved in Section VI by simulation, not in Section V).
Even with ZF precoding, small-cell systems perform WORSE than cell-free
because:
  (a) You use only K APs out of M, wasting M − K of them.
  (b) The selected K APs have limited diversity — if all happen to be
      far from one particular user, that user is doomed.
  (c) ZF with K APs forcing K users has ZERO degrees of freedom left
      for beamforming gain. Cell-free ZF uses M APs forcing K users,
      leaving M − K + 1 degrees of freedom for array gain.

The small-cell analysis therefore serves one purpose: to give a fair
comparison baseline that honors max-min fairness (even the small-cell
system uses optimal power control). After reading Section V, you can
rigorously interpret the 5×–10× gains reported in Section VI.

2.1  SYSTEM SETUP AND GREEDY AP-TO-USER ASSIGNMENT (Eq. 22)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Same geographical scenario as the cell-free case:
  • M single-antenna APs and K users randomly placed in the same area.
  • Same channel model g_mk = √(β_mk) · h_mk.
  • Same pilot-based MMSE channel estimation (Section II).

KEY DIFFERENCE: each AP can only serve ONE user. So we need a user-to-AP
assignment rule. Let m_k denote the AP assigned to user k, where m_k ∈
{1, …, M} and all m_k are distinct (no two users share the same AP).

Finding the OPTIMAL assignment (the K-permutation of APs that maximizes
some system objective, like min rate) is a combinatorial problem — it
involves searching over K-out-of-M permutations, which is O(M!/(M−K)!).
For M = 128, K = 16, that's ~2.6×10³² possibilities. Prohibitive.

GREEDY HEURISTIC (Eq. 22):

       m_k  =  argmax_{n : AP_n is available}  β_{n, k}             (22)

Assignment procedure:
   1. Sort users in some order (order doesn't matter much).
   2. For each user k in order: assign to the user the available AP with
      the LARGEST large-scale fading coefficient β_{n, k}, i.e., the
      closest-signal-strength AP from those not yet taken.
   3. Mark the chosen AP as no longer available.

This is the "closest AP" heuristic, a standard baseline in small-cell
literature. It is NOT optimal (it may cause conflicts where two users
both want the same closest AP) but it is simple and gives a clear
comparison point.

The remaining M − K APs are IDLE — they transmit nothing.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  MATCHED-FILTER (MF) RECEIVER (Eqs. 23, 24)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Each active AP m_k transmits ONLY user k's data symbol, scaled by a power
coefficient √η_k. There is NO precoding (no phase pre-rotation) at the
AP. The transmitted signal is:

       x_{m_k}  =  √(ρ_f · η_k)  ·  s_k.

User k receives a superposition of its own AP's signal plus leakage from
other active APs that are serving other users:

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f η_k) · g_{m_k, k} · s_k
              +  Σᵢ≠k  √(ρ_f η_i) · g_{m_i, k} · s_i
              +  w_k.                                               (23)

The first term is user k's desired signal (passed through the direct
channel g_{m_k, k}); the second term is interference from all other
active APs; w_k ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1) is receiver noise.

MATCHED-FILTER RECEIVER. User k processes y_k by multiplying by the
conjugate of its channel estimate from AP m_k, then normalizing:

       ŝ_k  =  (ĝ*_{m_k, k} / |ĝ_{m_k, k}|) · y_k.

This is just the classical MRC (Maximum-Ratio Combining) filter for a
SISO link with known channel estimate. It maximizes the post-processing
SNR for a Gaussian noise-plus-interference model.

CRITICALLY, user k must know ĝ_{m_k, k}. Unlike cell-free (where users
only know statistics), here the user is served by a single AP and gets
the instantaneous channel estimate via a downlink training signal or
error-free feedback from the AP. This is consistent with the traditional
cellular architecture.

2.3  ACHIEVABLE-RATE FORMULA (Eq. 24) — THE Ei(·) APPEARS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STARTING POINT. The post-filter SINR at user k is:

                        ρ_f · η_k · |ĝ_{m_k, k}|²
       SINR_k  =  ─────────────────────────────────────────────     (24′)
                   1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · β_{m_i, k}  −  ρ_f η_k γ_{m_k, k}

Let me unpack the denominator carefully:

  • "1"                            — noise variance.
  • ρ_f Σᵢ η_i β_{m_i, k}          — total power received at user k from
                                     all K active APs (using the true
                                     channel power β). This is the TOTAL
                                     received signal + interference.
  • − ρ_f η_k γ_{m_k, k}           — subtract the ESTIMATED part of the
                                     own signal. What remains is the
                                     mean-square unknown component of
                                     the direct signal PLUS all
                                     interference + noise.

Rearranging:
       Denom  =  1  +  ρ_f · η_k · (β_{m_k, k} − γ_{m_k, k})
                   +  ρ_f · Σᵢ≠k  η_i · β_{m_i, k}.

The first correction term is the channel estimation error variance of
the own direct link (AP m_k to user k). The second is interference from
other APs. This is the standard "MF SINR with imperfect CSI" form.

SUBTLETY. Unlike cell-free CB and ZF, the SINR depends on |ĝ_{m_k, k}|²,
which is a RANDOM QUANTITY (no channel hardening — only one AP). The
achievable rate must be computed by taking the expectation over this
random quantity:

       E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)].

Since |ĝ_{m_k, k}|² is the squared modulus of a 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_{m_k, k})
variable, it is exponentially distributed with mean γ_{m_k, k}. Define:

       Z  :=  |ĝ_{m_k, k}|² · ρ_f · η_k / (denominator without |ĝ|² part).

Then SINR_k = Z · γ_{m_k, k}^{-1} · E[|ĝ_{m_k, k}|²]·Z ... hmm, let me
just write it out cleanly.

Let
       a  :=  ρ_f · η_k · γ_{m_k, k},
       b  :=  1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ≠k η_i · β_{m_i, k} + ρ_f η_k (β_{m_k, k} − γ_{m_k, k}).

Rewrite SINR_k as:
       SINR_k  =  (a/γ_{m_k, k}) · |ĝ_{m_k, k}|²  /  b.

Let U := |ĝ_{m_k, k}|² / γ_{m_k, k} ~ Exponential(1). Then:

       SINR_k  =  (a/b) · U,    U ~ Exp(1).

Expected rate:
       E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)]
     =  ∫₀^∞  log₂(1 + (a/b) · u)  ·  e^(−u)  du.

Substitute v := (a/b) · u, so u = (b/a) · v, du = (b/a) dv, e^{-u} =
e^{-bv/a}:

       =  ∫₀^∞  log₂(1 + v)  ·  e^{-bv/a}  ·  (b/a)  dv
       =  (1/ln 2) · ∫₀^∞  ln(1 + v)  ·  (b/a) · e^{-(b/a)v}  dv.

The integral ∫₀^∞ ln(1 + v) · λ e^{-λv} dv (with λ = b/a) has a closed
form in terms of the exponential integral:

       ∫₀^∞ ln(1 + v) · λ e^{-λv} dv  =  e^λ · E₁(λ),

where E₁(λ) = ∫_λ^∞ (e^{-t}/t) dt is the (variant of the) exponential
integral. (Warning: there are several conventions for "Ei(·)" in the
literature. The paper uses the definition

       Ei(x)  =  ∫_x^∞ (e^{-t}/t) dt,

which is what some books call E₁(x). This matches the Gradshteyn-
Ryzhik formula 4.337.1 and 4.337.5.)

So:
       E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)]  =  (1/ln 2) · e^{b/a} · Ei(b/a).

Define α_k := b/a:

       α_k  =  (1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i β_{m_i, k}  −  ρ_f η_k γ_{m_k, k})
                 /  (ρ_f · η_k · γ_{m_k, k}).

(The numerator is the denominator of SINR_k, the denominator is the
"useful-signal coefficient".)

Then:
       E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)]  =  (1/ln 2) · e^{α_k} · Ei(α_k)         (24)

which is exactly the paper's Eq. (24).

INTERPRETATION OF α_k.
  • α_k is SMALL when: the direct channel estimation variance γ_{m_k,k}
    is large (close AP) AND interference is small (sparse user geometry)
    AND η_k is close to 1 (full power).
  • α_k is LARGE when: the direct channel is weak, interference is
    heavy, or η_k is small.
  • The function (1/ln 2) · e^x · Ei(x) is MONOTONIC DECREASING in x.
    So smaller α_k ⟹ larger achievable rate.

This is the small-cell MF achievable rate under imperfect CSI and MUI.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  MAX-MIN MF POWER ALLOCATION (Eq. 25)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The optimization problem:

       maximize_η    min_{k ∈ {1,…,K}}  E[log₂(1 + SINR_k(η))]       (25a)

       subject to    0  ≤  η_k  ≤  1,    k = 1, …, K.                (25b)

A few points:

(i) The constraint 0 ≤ η_k ≤ 1 is the per-AP power constraint — each
    active AP can only transmit up to full power (η_k = 1 means AP m_k
    transmits at ρ_f). Note there are ONLY K constraints, not M, because
    only K APs are active.

(ii) The objective is a min of monotonic functions of α_k, which are
     themselves ratio-of-linear functions of η. The paper states (without
     full proof, but with the remark "similar to (17)") that this is
     QUASI-CONCAVE in η. The argument is that each SINR_k(η) has a
     super-level-set that is convex, because
        SINR_k(η) ≥ t ⟺ b ≤ (a/t) ⟺ 1 + ρ_f Σᵢ≠k η_i β_{m_i,k} + ρ_f η_k (β_{m_k,k}−γ_{m_k,k})
                                    ≤ (ρ_f η_k γ_{m_k,k})/t
     which is a LINEAR constraint in η. Hence the feasibility set is a
     polytope, and the problem is a max-min LP in t after bisection.

(iii) Equivalently, since (1/ln 2) e^α Ei(α) is monotonic decreasing in
      α, maximizing min_k rate is equivalent to minimizing max_k α_k
      (i.e., equalize the α's).

(iv) A LEMMA AND COROLLARY SIMILAR TO LEMMA 1 AND COROLLARY 1 hold for
     (25). The paper states this without giving a separate proof. The
     reasoning: the structure of the feasibility problem (linear in η
     for fixed t) is identical to the ZF case, and the same Perron-
     Frobenius / M-matrix argument applies. Hence feasibility can be
     checked by solving K LINEAR EQUATIONS (not an SOCP), yielding the
     same O(K³) per-iteration cost.

2.5  ZF PRECODER FOR SMALL CELLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now the authors consider a more sophisticated small-cell variant: the K
selected APs share their channel estimates over a backhaul and jointly
form a ZF precoding matrix to cancel inter-user interference at those
K APs. This is essentially a "mini cell-free ZF" restricted to the K
active APs.

NOTATION.
  Let m_1, …, m_K be the K selected APs (from Eq. 22).
  Let Ĝ ∈ ℂ^{K×K} be the SQUARE channel estimate matrix:
       [Ĝ]_{i, k}  =  ĝ_{m_i, k}.
  Note that Ĝ is K×K (not M×K), because only the selected APs participate.
  Assume Ĝ is full rank (typically true with probability 1).

ZF PRECODING MATRIX (for small-cell ZF):

       A  =  (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P,

where P = diag(√η_1, …, √η_K).

This is DIFFERENT from the cell-free ZF expression Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P. Why?
Because when Ĝ is square, (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ = (Ĝᵀ · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹)⁻¹ simplifies.
Actually: for a square invertible matrix, the pseudo-inverse is just
the inverse. So A = Ĝ* (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ P = (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ P for a square Ĝ.

VERIFICATION. Check that ĜᵀA = P (diagonal), i.e., no interference:
       Ĝᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P  =  I_K · P  =  P.  ✓

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  RECEIVED SIGNAL AT USER k (Eq. 26)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Writing the user-k received signal after the K selected APs jointly
transmit with the ZF precoder:

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f) · g_kᵀ · A · s  +  w_k
            =  √(ρ_f) · (ĝ_k + ~g_k)ᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P · s  +  w_k,

where g_k = (g_{m_1, k}, …, g_{m_K, k})ᵀ is the vector of channels from
all K active APs to user k.

Note that ĝ_kᵀ = the k-th row of Ĝᵀ, and ĝ_kᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ = eᵀ_k. So:

       √(ρ_f) · ĝ_kᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P · s  =  √(ρ_f) · eᵀ_k · P · s
                                         =  √(ρ_f · η_k) · s_k.

The remaining term involves ~g_kᵀ:

       √(ρ_f) · ~g_kᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P · s,

which depends on s_1, …, s_K because ~g_k is generally not orthogonal
to the other rows of (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹.

Putting it together (Eq. 26):

       y_k  =  √(ρ_f · η_k) · s_k    (J_0: signal)
              +  √(ρ_f) · ~g_kᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ · P · s    (J_1: error leak)
              +  w_k.                                               (26)

This is STRUCTURALLY IDENTICAL to the cell-free ZF decomposition (Eq. 12),
except the matrix size is K×K instead of M×K. Same Theorem 1 proof
applies.

2.7  THEOREM 2 — SMALL-CELL ZF ACHIEVABLE RATE (Appendix G)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STATEMENT. An achievable rate of the k-th user in the small-cell ZF
scheme is log₂(1 + SINR_{k,ZF}), where:

                           ρ_f · η_k
       SINR_{k,ZF}  =  ────────────────────────                     (27a)
                        1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{ki}

and ν_{ki} is the i-th element of:

       ν_k  =  diag{ 𝔼[ (Ĝ)⁻¹ · 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ ] }         (27)

with 𝔼[~g_k ~g_kᵀ] being a K×K diagonal matrix having on its i-th diagonal
entry the value:

       β_{m_i, k}  −  (ρ_r τ · β²_{m_i, k}) / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_{m_i, k})
       =  β_{m_i, k}  −  γ_{m_i, k}.

Compare this to the cell-free ZF ν_k (Eq. 14):

       ν_k^{cell-free}  =  diag{ 𝔼[ (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · Ĝᵀ · 𝔼[~g_k~g_kᵀ] · Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ ] }

The small-cell version is SIMPLER because Ĝ is square — there's no
pseudoinverse, just (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹. The formulas are structurally analogous but
the small-cell version operates on a K×K system while the cell-free
version operates on an M×K system (with M ≫ K).

PROOF (Appendix G). The proof is a carbon copy of Appendix C (cell-free
Theorem 1), with Ĝ replaced by Ĝ (square) and with appropriate matrix
identities:

(i) Uncorrelated terms J_0, J_1, w_k (verified by independence of data
    symbols, channel estimates, and noise).

(ii) Hassibi-Hochwald worst-case uncorrelated-noise bound:
       SINR_{k,ZF}  =  𝔼[|J_0|²] / (1 + 𝔼[|J_1|²]).

(iii) 𝔼[|J_0|²] = ρ_f · η_k.

(iv) 𝔼[|J_1|²] = ρ_f · 𝔼[tr( P² · (Ĝᵀ)⁻ᴴ · ~g*_k · ~g_kᵀ · (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ )]
                = ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{ki},
     with ν_k as in (27).

2.8  PER-AP TRANSMIT POWER — INSTANTANEOUS VS. AVERAGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Here small-cell ZF diverges from cell-free ZF in an important way. In
cell-free, every per-AP power constraint was taken as the EXPECTATION
𝔼[|x_m|²], giving a smooth constraint on the power coefficients. In
small-cell, because there are only K APs each doing a critical job, the
paper considers BOTH instantaneous and average constraints.

DEFINITION. Let αᵀ_{[m_1]}, …, αᵀ_{[m_K]} be the rows of (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹. Define:

       ν_{m_k}  =  diag{ α_{[m_k]}* · αᵀ_{[m_k]} }.

Here ν_{m_k} is a K-vector with i-th component |[α_{[m_k]}]_i|², which
is the "instantaneous cost per η_i at AP m_k". It is a RANDOM QUANTITY
(depends on the small-scale fading h).

The INSTANTANEOUS transmit power from AP m_k is:

       |x_{m_k}|²  =  ρ_f · | Σᵢ [α_{[m_k]}]_i · √η_i · s_i |²
                    ≈  ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · |[α_{[m_k]}]_i|²  (data symbols uncorrelated)
                    =  ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · ν_{m_k, i}.

Two options for the per-AP power constraint:

(A) INSTANTANEOUS CONSTRAINT (Eq. 28):

       Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · ν_{m_k, i}  ≤  1,   k = 1, …, K.                (28b)

  This must hold for EVERY channel realization. Because ν_{m_k} is a
  random quantity, the constraint is realization-by-realization. Since
  the ν_{m_k, i} will fluctuate with h, a static η must satisfy the
  WORST-CASE realization, which is tight.

(B) AVERAGE CONSTRAINT (Eq. 29):

       Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ η_i · 𝔼[ν_{m_k, i}]  ≤  1,   k = 1, …, K.             (29b)

  Here ν̄_{m_k, i} := 𝔼[ν_{m_k, i}] (expectation over h) is deterministic
  given β. The constraint only holds on average across fading
  realizations, which is looser and hence allows larger η.

PHYSICAL MEANING. Instantaneous constraints correspond to hardware that
cannot tolerate momentary excess power — peak-power-limited amplifiers.
Average constraints correspond to RMS / thermal limits that only care
about time-averaged power.

BOTH PROBLEMS ARE QUASI-LINEAR. They fit the same framework as (17), and
Lemma 1 / Corollary 1 from Section IV-A apply directly to both. So
bisection + linear-equation feasibility check is the method.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.9  WHY INSTANTANEOUS OUTPERFORMS AVERAGE (FROM SECTION VI)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section VI's Figure 5 shows that:
  — Small-cell ZF with INSTANTANEOUS power constraints gives better
    5%-outage rate than small-cell ZF with AVERAGE power constraints.

Counterintuitive? One might expect average constraints (looser) to
allow higher throughput. But the explanation is:

  • Under average constraints, η_k can be chosen very large because the
    constraint only cares about the MEAN ν̄_{m_k, i}. For most fading
    realizations, the actual |x_{m_k}|² will then exceed 1 and cause
    "peak clipping" at the hardware.
  • The achievable rate formula implicitly assumes a LINEAR amplifier,
    but in reality nonlinear saturation distorts the transmitted waveform
    whenever the instantaneous power exceeds the PA limit.
  • The paper implicitly compares the two by taking their
    achievable-rate formulas at face value — under the average model,
    you get a higher nominal rate, but you pay in outage when rare
    fading realizations push the power too high.
  • At 5%-outage the rare events matter most, and average constraints
    "count" them as feasible even though in practice they would fail.

The instantaneous constraint is the "safer" engineering choice and it
turns out to outperform at the worst-case rate metric.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.10  A NEGATIVE ANSWER: ZF DOES NOT RESCUE SMALL CELLS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A reader might hope: "if cell-free ZF is great, and if small-cell ZF
is structurally similar, maybe small-cell ZF also works well". The
paper's answer (preview from Sec. VI, rigorously shown in Fig. 5-6) is
NO. Why?

  Reason 1: DEGREES OF FREEDOM. Cell-free ZF has M antennas forcing K
  users → (M − K + 1) remaining degrees of freedom for array gain, which
  is LARGE (say M − K = 112 for M = 128, K = 16). Small-cell ZF has
  exactly K antennas forcing K users → ZERO remaining degrees of freedom
  → no array gain, only interference nulling. The math is the same but
  the operating point is degenerate.

  Reason 2: AP UTILIZATION. Cell-free uses all M APs; small-cell uses
  only K. The factor of M/K in total radiated power and diversity
  matters a lot — especially in urban propagation with heavy shadowing.

  Reason 3: USER-AP MATCHING. Small-cell relies on the greedy
  assignment (22), which is suboptimal. Two users who both want the
  same nearby AP create a "collision" that forces one of them to use a
  far-away AP, tanking their rate. Cell-free has no such conflict — every
  AP serves every user.

Reason 1 is the decisive mathematical difference, and it is directly
visible in Fig. 5: small-cell ZF's SINR plateaus while cell-free ZF
continues to grow with M.

3.1  THE EXPONENTIAL INTEGRAL IN WIRELESS RATE FORMULAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The appearance of Ei(·) in Eq. (24) is typical whenever one computes
E[log(1 + |h|² · constant)] for a unit-exponential random variable |h|².
This integral pops up in:

  • Ergodic capacity of Rayleigh-fading SISO channels:
    I. E. Telatar, "Capacity of multi-antenna Gaussian channels,"
    Eur. Trans. Telecommun., vol. 10, no. 6, pp. 585–595, Nov./Dec. 1999.
  • M. S. Alouini, A. J. Goldsmith, "Capacity of Rayleigh fading channels
    under different adaptive transmission and diversity-combining
    techniques," IEEE Trans. Veh. Technol., vol. 48, no. 4,
    pp. 1165–1181, Jul. 1999. [Classical MRC/ergodic-capacity formulas
    with exponential integral.]
  • M. K. Simon, M. S. Alouini, Digital Communication over Fading
    Channels, Wiley, 2005. [Comprehensive treatment of MGF/PDF-based
    rate computations for fading.]
  • I. S. Gradshteyn, I. M. Ryzhik, Table of Integrals, Series, and
    Products, 8th ed., 2014. Formulas 4.337.1, 4.337.5 give
       ∫₀^∞ ln(1 + v) · e^{−λv} dv = (1/λ) · e^λ · E₁(λ)
    which is the precise identity used in deriving Eq. (24).

The Ei(x) is a well-studied special function with series and asymptotic
expansions:
  Ei(x)  ≈  −γ − ln(x) − Σₙ₌₁ (−x)ⁿ / (n · n!)      (small x)
        ≈  (e^{−x}/x) · [1 − 1/x + 2/x² − ⋯]        (large x, asymptotic)
where γ ≈ 0.5772 is Euler's constant. Standard libraries (SciPy,
MATLAB expint) compute it efficiently.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2  SMALL-CELL VS. CELL-FREE: THE SETTLED DEBATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The comparison of small-cell and cell-free (a.k.a. coordinated
multipoint, network MIMO, distributed antenna systems) has been a
recurring theme in the wireless literature:

  • Hoydis, Kobayashi, Debbah (2011) [reference [29] in the paper]:
    "Green small-cell networks," IEEE Veh. Technol. Mag., vol. 6, no. 1,
    pp. 37–43, Mar. 2011. Argued for small cells on energy-efficiency
    grounds.

  • Ngo, Ashikhmin, Yang, Larsson, Marzetta (2017) [reference [22]]:
    First systematic comparison of cell-free (CB) vs. small-cell (MF).
    Showed cell-free wins decisively at the 5%-outage metric.

  • Nayebi, Ashikhmin, Marzetta, Yang, Rao (2017) [THE PAPER]: Extended
    [22] with ZF (both cell-free and small-cell), proving that ZF does
    NOT close the gap.

  • Björnson, Sanguinetti, Debbah (2019) "Making Cell-Free Massive MIMO
    Competitive With MMSE Processing..." [the MMSE paper you've already
    studied]: Used MMSE combining instead of MF to reduce the gap
    between small-cell and cell-free in the uplink, showing that
    receiver processing matters.

  • Björnson, Sanguinetti (2020) "Scalable Cell-Free Massive MIMO
    Systems" [the scalable paper you've already studied]: Showed that
    cell-free CAN be scalable, closing a key practical concern.

The present paper's contribution sits at a key moment: it was the first
to comprehensively address ZF in both cell-free and small-cell, and to
compare them under fair per-antenna power constraints.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  ZF PSEUDO-INVERSE IN FULL-RANK SQUARE SYSTEMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When the channel matrix Ĝ is SQUARE (K×K with full rank), the ZF
pseudo-inverse reduces to the ordinary inverse (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹. This is the
"K-user, K-antenna" case studied as early as:

  • M. Costa, "Writing on dirty paper," IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory, vol. 29,
    no. 3, pp. 439–441, May 1983. [Dirty-Paper Coding achieves sum-
    capacity but is nonlinear.]
  • Q. H. Spencer, A. L. Swindlehurst, M. Haardt, "Zero-forcing methods
    for downlink spatial multiplexing in multiuser MIMO channels,"
    IEEE Trans. Signal Process., vol. 52, no. 2, pp. 461–471, Feb. 2004.
    [Classical K-user ZF analysis.]

Small-cell ZF, because Ĝ is square, inherits the "no extra degrees of
freedom" property from these K×K studies. The reason cell-free ZF works
so much better is entirely due to the M−K oversubscription.

5.1  EXAMPLE 1: COMPUTING α_k AND MF RATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Parameters (all normalized by noise variance, i.e., σ²_w = 1):
   K = 4 users, M = 32 APs (greedy assignment gives 4 active APs).
   ρ_f = 10 (max transmit power, normalized).
   Selected APs m_1, m_2, m_3, m_4.
   Slow fading coefficients (big β for own user, smaller for others):

       β_{m_1, 1} = 0.5,    β_{m_1, 2} = 0.02,   β_{m_1, 3} = 0.01,   β_{m_1, 4} = 0.005
       β_{m_2, 1} = 0.015,  β_{m_2, 2} = 0.4,    β_{m_2, 3} = 0.02,   β_{m_2, 4} = 0.01
       β_{m_3, 1} = 0.01,   β_{m_3, 2} = 0.03,   β_{m_3, 3} = 0.6,    β_{m_3, 4} = 0.015
       β_{m_4, 1} = 0.008,  β_{m_4, 2} = 0.012,  β_{m_4, 3} = 0.01,   β_{m_4, 4} = 0.45

Assume ρ_r · τ · β is large enough that γ_{m_k, k} ≈ β_{m_k, k} (perfect
estimation on the direct link). Assume full-power transmission η_1 =
η_2 = η_3 = η_4 = 1.

Compute α_1 for user 1:
   Numerator  =  1 + ρ_f · Σᵢ η_i · β_{m_i, 1}  −  ρ_f · η_1 · γ_{m_1, 1}
              =  1 + 10 · (1·0.5 + 1·0.015 + 1·0.01 + 1·0.008)  −  10 · 1 · 0.5
              =  1 + 10 · 0.533  −  5.0
              =  1 + 5.33  −  5.0
              =  1.33.

   Denominator  =  ρ_f · η_1 · γ_{m_1, 1}
               =  10 · 1 · 0.5
               =  5.0.

   α_1  =  1.33 / 5.0  =  0.266.

Compute E[log₂(1 + SINR_1)]:
   E[log₂(1 + SINR_1)]  =  (1/ln 2) · e^{0.266} · Ei(0.266).

   ln 2 ≈ 0.6931.
   e^{0.266} ≈ 1.3047.

   Ei(0.266): use series expansion Ei(x) = −γ − ln(x) − Σ (−x)ⁿ/(n·n!).
   For x = 0.266:
      −γ = −0.5772.
      −ln(0.266) = −(−1.324) = 1.324.
      First term of series: −(−0.266)/1 = 0.266.
      Second: +(0.266²)/(2·2) = 0.0708/4 = 0.0177.
      Third: −(0.266³)/(3·6) = −0.01883/18 = −0.00105.
      ...
      Ei(0.266) ≈ −0.5772 + 1.324 + 0.266 + 0.0177 − 0.00105 + ⋯
               ≈ 1.029.
   (More precisely, Ei(0.266) ≈ 1.029 using tables/SciPy.)

   E[log₂(1 + SINR_1)]  ≈  (1/0.6931) · 1.3047 · 1.029
                        ≈  1.4427 · 1.3047 · 1.029
                        ≈  1.937  bits/s/Hz.

By symmetry and similar calculations, users 2, 3, 4 would get similar
rates (slightly different because of their different β_{m_k, k} and
interference configurations). The minimum across users is the
performance indicator under max-min.

COMPARE to cell-free CB with the same parameters: the cell-free system
(128 APs, all serving all users) would give ~5-7 bits/s/Hz at the 5%-
outage level, about 3× better. This roughly matches Fig. 5 of the paper.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2  EXAMPLE 2: SMALL-CELL ZF WITH K=2
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Take K = 2 users, 2 selected APs. Channel estimate matrix (2×2):

       Ĝ = [ 2    0.3 ]        (row m_1, columns = users 1, 2)
           [ 0.4  1.8 ]        (row m_2)

       Ĝᵀ = [ 2    0.4 ]        (row user 1)
            [ 0.3  1.8 ]        (row user 2)

det(Ĝᵀ) = 2·1.8 − 0.4·0.3 = 3.6 − 0.12 = 3.48.

       (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ = (1/3.48) · [ 1.8   −0.4 ]
                            [−0.3    2   ]
              = [ 0.517   −0.115 ]
                [−0.0862   0.575 ]

Column 1 of (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ is (0.517, −0.0862)ᵀ. So |col 1|² = 0.267 + 0.00743 =
0.275.
Column 2 is (−0.115, 0.575)ᵀ. So |col 2|² = 0.01323 + 0.330 = 0.344.

(These columns are the per-AP precoding vectors for each user.)

NOW ν_{m_k} entries: the m_k-th row of (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ has elements |[⋯]_i|².
   Row m_1 (user 1) of (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹: (0.517, −0.115).
     ν_{m_1, 1} = 0.517² = 0.267.
     ν_{m_1, 2} = 0.115² = 0.01323.
   Row m_2 (user 2): (−0.0862, 0.575).
     ν_{m_2, 1} = 0.0862² = 0.00743.
     ν_{m_2, 2} = 0.575² = 0.330.

PER-AP CONSTRAINTS (Eq. 28):
   AP m_1:  0.267 · η_1 + 0.01323 · η_2  ≤  1.
   AP m_2:  0.00743 · η_1 + 0.330 · η_2  ≤  1.

Assume PERFECT CSI so ν_{ki} = 0 (for the SINR leakage), then SINR_k =
ρ_f · η_k and max-min gives η_1 = η_2 = η. Binding constraint:
   AP m_1:  0.280 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 3.57.
   AP m_2:  0.338 · η ≤ 1  ⟹  η ≤ 2.96.

Binding is AP m_2 (largest row sum). η^* = 2.96.
SINR = 10 · 2.96 = 29.6 (if ρ_f = 10), R = log₂(30.6) ≈ 4.93 bits/s/Hz.

COMPARISON TO MF ON SAME LINKS. With η_1 = η_2 = 1 under MF (no ZF), the
α_1 computation would yield a rate around 1.9 bits/s/Hz (as in Example 5.1).
So ZF over K = 2 APs looks much better (~4.93 > ~1.9).

CAUTION: These are NOT apples-to-apples — ZF has a different η range
(2.96 vs 1), and the toy example doesn't model real estimation error.
The important takeaway is the STRUCTURE: in small-cell ZF, the
bottleneck AP (largest row sum of ν_{m_k}) determines the performance,
just like Lemma 2 for cell-free ZF.

5.3  PRACTICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM. Consider a small-cell system with K = 3 users, and assume
perfect estimation (γ_{m_k, k} = β_{m_k, k}). Assume the selected APs'
direct-link β's are:

       β_{m_1, 1} = 0.8,
       β_{m_2, 2} = 0.6,
       β_{m_3, 3} = 0.5.

Inter-cell (cross) β's:
       β_{m_1, 2} = 0.05,    β_{m_1, 3} = 0.02,
       β_{m_2, 1} = 0.06,    β_{m_2, 3} = 0.03,
       β_{m_3, 1} = 0.02,    β_{m_3, 2} = 0.04.

ρ_f = 20 (normalized).

(a) Under MF with full power (η_1 = η_2 = η_3 = 1), compute α_1, α_2, α_3.
(b) Compute E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)] for each k.
(c) Which user has the lowest rate? What is the "minimum rate"?

SOLUTION.

(a) α_1:
    Numerator = 1 + ρ_f · (η_1 β_{m_1,1} + η_2 β_{m_2,1} + η_3 β_{m_3,1})
                − ρ_f · η_1 · γ_{m_1,1}
              = 1 + 20 · (0.8 + 0.06 + 0.02) − 20 · 0.8
              = 1 + 17.6 − 16
              = 2.6.
    Denominator = 20 · 0.8 = 16.
    α_1 = 2.6 / 16 = 0.1625.

    α_2:
    Numerator = 1 + 20 · (β_{m_1,2} + β_{m_2,2} + β_{m_3,2}) − 20 · β_{m_2,2}
              = 1 + 20 · (0.05 + 0.6 + 0.04) − 20 · 0.6
              = 1 + 13.8 − 12
              = 2.8.
    Denominator = 20 · 0.6 = 12.
    α_2 = 2.8 / 12 = 0.2333.

    α_3:
    Numerator = 1 + 20 · (0.02 + 0.03 + 0.5) − 20 · 0.5
              = 1 + 11.0 − 10
              = 2.0.
    Denominator = 20 · 0.5 = 10.
    α_3 = 2.0 / 10 = 0.2.

(b) Use E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)] = (1/ln 2) · e^{α_k} · Ei(α_k).

    Ei(0.1625) ≈ 1.486 (from tables / numerical).
    e^{0.1625} ≈ 1.1764.
    Rate_1 ≈ (1/0.6931) · 1.1764 · 1.486 ≈ 2.52 bits/s/Hz.

    Ei(0.2333) ≈ 1.148.
    e^{0.2333} ≈ 1.263.
    Rate_2 ≈ (1/0.6931) · 1.263 · 1.148 ≈ 2.09 bits/s/Hz.

    Ei(0.2) ≈ 1.223.
    e^{0.2} ≈ 1.221.
    Rate_3 ≈ (1/0.6931) · 1.221 · 1.223 ≈ 2.154 bits/s/Hz.

(c) User 2 has the lowest rate, ≈ 2.09 bits/s/Hz. Note that user 2 has
    the second-strongest direct link (β = 0.6) but the worst rate
    because it suffers the most interference from the strong AP m_1
    (β_{m_1, 2} = 0.05 is the largest cross-β on user 2). The minimum
    rate = 2.09 bits/s/Hz.

Observation: MF rates are insensitive to the relative order of direct
and cross β's because the "1" in the denominator dominates at this
power level. A max-min power allocation would reduce η_1 slightly
to reduce interference on user 2.

WHAT WE COVERED IN THIS SECTION:

  • Small-cell setup: K out of M APs active; each user served by exactly
    one AP. Greedy assignment rule Eq. (22) based on largest β_{n, k}.
  • Matched-Filter (MF) receiver operating mode:
      - Each AP transmits only its own user's symbol without precoding.
      - User applies MF ŝ_k = (ĝ*_{m_k, k}/|ĝ_{m_k, k}|) y_k.
      - Rate involves the exponential integral Ei(·) because there is
        no channel hardening.
      - Eq. (24): E[log₂(1 + SINR_k)] = (1/ln 2) · e^{α_k} · Ei(α_k).
  • MF max-min power allocation Eq. (25), quasi-concave, solved by
    bisection + linear-equation feasibility (Lemma 1 / Corollary 1
    analog).
  • Small-cell ZF precoder (mini-cell-free version on K APs):
      - Precoding matrix A = (Ĝᵀ)⁻¹ P (square, no pseudoinverse).
      - Received signal decomposition Eq. (26).
      - Theorem 2 (Appendix G): SINR formula Eq. (27), analogous to
        cell-free Theorem 1.
  • Two per-AP power constraint variants:
      - INSTANTANEOUS (Eq. 28): Σᵢ η_i ν_{m_k, i} ≤ 1 each realization.
      - AVERAGE (Eq. 29): Σᵢ η_i ν̄_{m_k, i} ≤ 1 on average.
    Both are quasi-linear, solved by bisection + Corollary 1.
  • Preview of Section VI result: small-cell ZF does NOT outperform
    small-cell MF at 5%-outage, and both are beaten by cell-free ZF by
    5-10×.

KEY TAKEAWAYS:

  1. Small cells use only K out of M APs; this wastes M − K APs worth
     of diversity and array gain.
  2. The MF achievable rate depends on |ĝ_{m_k, k}|² (random), requiring
     an expectation over fading and yielding the Ei(·) formula. This is
     fundamentally different from cell-free where channel hardening
     replaces the random quantity by its mean.
  3. The small-cell ZF precoder has a square Ĝ, so zero degrees of
     freedom remain after canceling inter-user interference — no array
     gain is accumulated.
  4. The Lemma 1 / Corollary 1 machinery from Section IV-A carries over
     directly to small-cell problems (25), (28), (29), giving them the
     same O(K³) per-bisection cost.
  5. The instantaneous power constraint is the safer engineering choice;
     at the 5%-outage metric it outperforms the average constraint.
