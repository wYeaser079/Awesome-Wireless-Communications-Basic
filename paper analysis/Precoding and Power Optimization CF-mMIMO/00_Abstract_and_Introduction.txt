PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Imagine a town where you want every person, regardless of where they stand, to
get the same good mobile signal. The traditional way is to build a few very tall
towers ("cells"), and every person gets stuck talking to the tower closest to
them. The problem is: the person standing right under the tower gets an amazing
signal, but the person near the edge of the coverage area gets a terrible one.
This unfairness is the biggest complaint about cellular networks.

Massive MIMO is one attempt to fix this. Instead of one antenna on a tower, you
put HUNDREDS of antennas on it. With so many antennas, the tower can "aim" its
signal precisely at each user simultaneously. This helps a lot, but the user at
the edge of the cell still suffers from:
  (a) long distance to the tower,
  (b) interference from neighbouring towers.

The paper studies a very different idea called "Cell-Free Massive MIMO". Now,
instead of concentrating all antennas on one tower, you SPRINKLE hundreds of
small, cheap, single-antenna "access points" (APs) all over the town — on
lampposts, on buildings, anywhere — and connect them all to a single brain
(called the network controller, NC). The network is NOT divided into cells.
Every AP in the town serves EVERY user at the same time, using coordinated
transmissions orchestrated by the central brain.

Two questions dominate this system:
  Question 1: HOW should each AP shape its signal so that the signals from
              all APs add up constructively at the intended user and cancel
              destructively at everyone else? This is called "precoding". The
              paper studies two popular precoders:
               • Conjugate Beamforming (CB)  — simple, distributed.
               • Zero-Forcing (ZF)           — more complex, but kills
                                                multi-user interference.

  Question 2: HOW much power should each AP spend on each user? If every AP
              blasts at full power, it wastes energy and creates interference
              between users. If it's too quiet, users don't hear it. This is
              "power allocation". The authors use a "max-min" rule: find the
              power budget that makes the WORST user as happy as possible. This
              is the fairness principle — raise the floor for the weakest user
              instead of making strong users stronger.

The paper's headline findings, in plain English:
  1. Cell-Free Massive MIMO beats "small-cell" systems (where each user only
     talks to one AP) by a factor of 5× to 10× in terms of what the WORST
     5% of users experience.
  2. Zero-Forcing is much better than Conjugate Beamforming in a cell-free
     network with good central coordination.
  3. Counterintuitively, when you do optimal max-min power control, MOST of
     the APs end up transmitting at LESS than their full power. This is
     strange: you'd expect that in a fairness-seeking scheme, everybody would
     push their transmit button as hard as possible. But the math says no —
     APs close to a user should actually transmit GENTLY, because they don't
     need to shout, and shouting only creates interference for others.
  4. Because exact max-min power control is computationally expensive, the
     authors design simple "shortcut" algorithms (heuristics) that almost
     match the optimal performance but run much faster, which is what a real
     network would need.

The whole paper is, at heart, a numerical and mathematical study of: "If I
want to give uniformly good service to everyone in a big area, what are the
best precoding + power allocation combinations in this new architecture?"

PART 2 — DETAILED, TECHNICAL (PhD-LEVEL) EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The Introduction section serves six distinct technical purposes, which we'll
dissect one at a time.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1  HISTORICAL POSITIONING: from Shannon-era MIMO to Cell-Free Massive MIMO
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To properly understand where Cell-Free Massive MIMO sits in the intellectual
lineage of wireless communications, we have to trace the evolution in three
phases.

PHASE A — CLASSICAL MULTI-USER MIMO (early 2000s).
The broadcast-channel capacity results of Caire–Shamai (2003) [5],
Viswanath–Tse (2003) [6], and Vishwanath–Jindal–Goldsmith (2003) [7]
established that the capacity of a Gaussian MIMO broadcast channel is
achieved by *Dirty-Paper Coding* (DPC), a clever non-linear scheme in which
the transmitter "pre-subtracts" interference known at the transmitter but not
at the receiver. Two crucial assumptions underpinned these results:

      (i)  Both transmitter and receiver perfectly know the channel.
      (ii) Dirty-Paper Coding (non-linear, computationally heavy) is feasible.

These assumptions gave a beautiful theory but an impractical roadmap, because
DPC becomes computationally intractable as antenna counts grow and perfect
CSI at the transmitter is unrealistic in FDD systems.

PHASE B — MASSIVE MIMO (Marzetta 2010).
Marzetta's landmark 2010 paper [2] reversed the direction of thought: instead
of trying to achieve capacity with perfect CSI and DPC, *scale up* the number
of base-station antennas M so that even very simple, sub-optimal tools
(linear precoding like MRT/ZF, channel reciprocity via TDD, MMSE channel
estimation from uplink pilots) already give close-to-capacity performance.
The mechanism behind this miracle is *channel hardening* — when the number
of base-station antennas M → ∞, the random channel vector g_k between the
base station and user k concentrates tightly around its mean, so that the
effective gain ‖g_k‖²/M → β_k deterministically. Fast fading vanishes at
the array, inter-user interference becomes predictable, and simple linear
methods approach the information-theoretic limits. This scalability with M
is the defining feature of Massive MIMO.

PHASE C — DISTRIBUTED / NETWORK / CELL-FREE MIMO (2010–present).
The natural question became: "Why should all M antennas sit at one physical
location?" If you instead spread them out across the geographical coverage
area, you get (1) much shorter distances on average between APs and users,
(2) stronger diversity against shadowing, and (3) uniform coverage.

Several lines of prior work explored this, using different names and
assumptions — distributed antenna systems (DAS), network MIMO, cooperative
multi-cell beamforming, coordinated multipoint (CoMP):

     [10] Gesbert et al.   — multi-cell MIMO cooperative networks survey.
     [11] Sawahashi et al. — CoMP in LTE-Advanced.
     [12] Joung et al.     — large-scale DAS for energy efficiency.
     [13] Lozano et al.    — fundamental limits of cooperation.
     [14] Huang et al.     — distributed beamforming with limited coordination.
     [15] Somekh et al.    — cooperative multi-cell ZF in cellular downlink.
     [16] Huh et al.       — large-system analysis of network MIMO with ZF.
     [17] Truong & Heath   — viability of distributed antennas for Massive MIMO.
     [18] Yang & Marzetta  — multi-cell Massive MIMO capacity bounds.
     [19] Wang & Dai       — asymptotic rate analysis for distributed antennas.
     [20] Yin, Gesbert & Cottatellucci — statistical interference handling.

Each of these studies, however, retained a "cell" abstraction or focused on
sum-rate, not per-user fairness. The term "Cell-Free Massive MIMO" was coined
by Ngo, Ashikhmin, Yang, Larsson, and Marzetta in their 2015 SPAWC paper [21]
and then expanded in the 2017 TWC paper "Cell-Free Massive MIMO versus Small
Cells" [22]. These are the direct intellectual ancestors of the present work.
The key distinction of cell-free massive MIMO versus prior distributed-antenna
literature is:

      • No cell partition and no user association rules — all APs serve all
        users jointly.
      • TDD-based operation, exploiting uplink/downlink channel reciprocity
        to avoid feedback overhead.
      • Simple linear precoding (CB or ZF), not DPC.
      • Performance metric = per-user rate (especially 5%-outage), NOT
        sum-rate — the focus is uniform quality-of-service.

The present Nayebi et al. 2017 paper plugs into this exact lineage and asks:
"Given that we have cell-free massive MIMO with CB and ZF, what is the best
power control strategy, and how large are the complexity/performance
trade-offs?" That is its contribution slot.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  CONTRAST WITH Ngo et al. 2017 [22] — WHY A NEW PAPER?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The reader should understand precisely what [22] (Ngo et al., "Cell-Free
Massive MIMO versus Small Cells", IEEE TWC 2017) did and what this paper
adds. Specifically:

     [22] analyzed:
        • Only conjugate beamforming (CB) for cell-free.
        • Only matched-filter (MF) receiver for small cells (no ZF).
        • Proposed a max-min power control for CB that requires solving a
          non-convex optimization of high computational complexity (through
          iterative SOCP / geometric programming).
        • Focused heavily on pilot assignment to mitigate pilot contamination.

     Nayebi et al. (this paper) adds:
        (a) Zero-Forcing (ZF) precoder for cell-free, including a new
            capacity lower bound (their Theorem 1).
        (b) A low-complexity near-optimal power control for ZF derived from
            a perfect-CSI analytical solution (their Eq. (21)).
        (c) Three novel low-complexity heuristic power allocations for CB
            (full power, heuristic fixed-power, heuristic uniform
            coefficients — Sections III-B, III-C, III-D).
        (d) ZF precoder extended to the small-cell architecture (Section V)
            as a fair comparison baseline.
        (e) A counterintuitive finding and empirical characterization: at
            the optimal max-min point, most APs transmit at *less* than
            full power, and APs closer to users transmit with *smaller*
            power (Figure 3 in Section VI).

Critically, [22] used pilot contamination as a central concern because it
allowed users to potentially share pilots. Nayebi et al. explicitly assume
LOW MOBILITY (< 10 km/h, pedestrians), which dramatically increases the
coherence interval τ_c. Since τ_c ≫ K (number of users), every user gets a
fresh orthogonal pilot, so pilot contamination becomes negligible. This
assumption isolates the precoding+power-allocation question from the pilot
assignment question and allows the authors to focus purely on the max-min
optimization problem.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3  PERFORMANCE CRITERIA: WHY 5%-OUTAGE AND NOT SUM-RATE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A crucial methodological contribution is the insistence on *per-user fairness
metrics*, rather than sum throughput. Two concrete metrics are used:

     (i) Minimum rate among all users:
                R_min = min_{k} R_k

     (ii) 5%-outage rate:
                The smallest rate among the best 95% of users,
                i.e., R_{5%-outage} = F_R⁻¹(0.05)
                where F_R is the CDF of the rate distribution.

The authors justify this choice philosophically: in 5G/6G and beyond, the
service paradigm is not "how much total data can I pipe through the system"
but "what is the worst experience a user can have?" A bar where ONE user
gets 1 Gbps while another gets 10 kbps can be sum-rate-optimal but is a
product failure.

A subtle but important remark: MAX-MIN optimization makes all users equal,
so R_min and R_{5%-outage} become the same quantity at the optimum, for a
given channel realization. The empirical CDF over realizations still shows a
spread (due to different geometric configurations from random user/AP
dropping). So the authors use max-min as an *engineering tool* — it is NOT
provably optimal for 5%-outage, but in practice it works well and gives
uniformly good service.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  THE MAIN TECHNICAL CONTRIBUTIONS (paraphrased and enumerated)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Reading carefully between lines 152–169 of the Introduction, the explicit
list of contributions is:

  C1. Quantitative demonstration that cell-free provides 5×–10× gain over
      small-cell in 5%-outage rate.
  C2. ZF precoder significantly outperforms CB in the cell-free architecture.
  C3. Counterintuitive empirical observation: at the max-min optimum for CB,
      most APs transmit at less than full power, and their transmit power
      decreases with their proximity to the strongest served user.
  C4. Low-complexity heuristic power allocations for CB motivated by the
      above observation (exponential fitting of p_m vs. β_m^max).
  C5. A suboptimal but near-optimal closed-form power allocation for ZF,
      derived from the perfect-CSI case and proven to be ε-optimal for
      sufficiently large uplink pilot power ρ_r (Proposition 2).
  C6. Analysis of small-cell systems with both MF and ZF, and power
      allocation algorithms for both.
  C7. Capacity lower bounds for cell-free systems with both CB and ZF,
      accounting for:
          — channel estimation error (the term T3 in Appendix A),
          — lack of channel knowledge at users via channel hardening
            (the term T2),
          — multi-user interference (T1),
          — noise (w_k).
      This gives a rigorous achievable-rate formula usable in the power
      optimization problem formulation.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  SYSTEM ASSUMPTIONS (collected)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

By the end of the Introduction, the following assumptions are on the table
(reinforced in Section II):

  A1. M ≫ K (far more APs than users), consistent with Massive-MIMO ethos.
  A2. Each AP has a single antenna (simplifies hardware; justifies the
      "distributed" interpretation).
  A3. TDD protocol, exploiting channel reciprocity.
  A4. Low user mobility (≤ 10 km/h) → long coherence block → orthogonal
      pilots available for all users → no pilot contamination.
  A5. NC (Network Controller) knows all large-scale fading coefficients
      β_mk for all m ∈ {1,…,M}, k ∈ {1,…,K}.
  A6. Large-scale fading changes about 40× slower than small-scale fading
      [25], making β_mk quasi-static. This minimizes backhaul traffic for
      power-control coefficient updates.
  A7. Flat-fading per OFDM subcarrier; β_mk is frequency-independent.
  A8. Geographic area is small enough that propagation-time differences
      between any two APs to a given user are within the OFDM cyclic prefix
      — this justifies treating all APs as synchronously contributing to a
      single OFDM symbol at each user.

PART 3 — MATHEMATICAL DETAILS (Unicode format)
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The Introduction does not contain full derivations, but it does set the
foundational symbols and the structure of the SINR / rate expressions that
are referenced downstream. Here we establish and explain them.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1  CHANNEL MODEL (Eq. (1) in Section II, previewed here)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The channel between AP m and user k:

        g_mk  =  √(β_mk) · h_mk                                      (1)

where:
  β_mk ∈ ℝ₊   = large-scale fading coefficient (path loss + shadowing).
               Slowly varying; depends on distance and shadowing.
  h_mk ∈ ℂ    = small-scale (Rayleigh) fading, h_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1).
               i.i.d. across (m, k) and across coherence blocks.

Interpretation: the slow variable β_mk is treated as quasi-deterministic and
known at the NC; the random part h_mk is what needs to be estimated each
coherence block.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2  RATE / SINR STRUCTURE (to be used in all sections)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Throughout the paper, the achievable rate of user k will take the form:

        R_k  =  log₂(1 + SINR_k)                                     (★)

where SINR_k will be different for CB vs. ZF vs. small-cell. All are derived
under the "worst-case uncorrelated noise" argument (Hassibi–Hochwald 2003
[30]), which states:

        "Given the transmitted signal decomposition
            y_k = T₀ · s_k + Σᵢ (interfering terms) + noise,
         where T₀, the interference terms, and the noise are mutually
         uncorrelated, treating them as independent Gaussian noise gives a
         valid lower bound on the achievable rate."

This is the tool that enables the *closed-form capacity lower bounds* used
as the OBJECTIVE of the max-min optimization problems in Sections III and IV.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.3  MAX-MIN OPTIMIZATION: the canonical form
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The generic max-min power allocation problem studied in this paper has the
form:

        maximize_{η}         min_{k ∈ {1,…,K}} SINR_k(η)            (P)

        subject to           per-antenna transmit-power constraints
                             on each AP m ∈ {1,…,M}:

                             Σᵢ η_mi · [something β]  ≤  1

Here η = (η_mk) is the collection of power-control coefficients, one per
(AP, user) pair. These problems are QUASI-CONCAVE (in the CB case) or
QUASI-LINEAR (in the ZF case), both of which are amenable to the BISECTION
METHOD from convex optimization — see Boyd & Vandenberghe [26, Ch. 4.2.5].

The bisection strategy works as follows (foreshadowed in the Introduction
and detailed in Section III-A):

  1. Choose an interval (t_ℓ, t_u) known to contain the optimal SINR target t*.
  2. At iteration j, set t_j = (t_ℓ + t_u)/2 and check whether the feasibility
     problem
                 find η
                 s.t.  SINR_k(η) ≥ t_j ∀k
                       Σᵢ η_mi · β's  ≤ 1  ∀m
     is feasible. This is a convex feasibility problem because for fixed t
     the set {η : SINR_k(η) ≥ t} is convex (due to quasi-concavity).
  3. If feasible, update t_ℓ ← t_j; else update t_u ← t_j.
  4. Stop when |t_u − t_ℓ| < ε.

The computational cost is what motivates the entire "heuristic algorithms"
agenda of Sections III-B, III-C, III-D and IV-B. Every convex feasibility
check costs O(polynomial(M·K)), and bisection requires log₂(1/ε) iterations.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.4  THE TWO PRECODERS IN ONE LINE EACH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Introduction names both precoders; their formal expressions (from
Sections III, IV) are:

  • CONJUGATE BEAMFORMING (CB) at AP m:

        x_m  =  √(ρ_f) · Σ_{i=1}^{K} √(η_mi) · ĝ_mi* · s_i               (3)

      — i.e., each AP multiplies the data symbol by the CONJUGATE of its
        channel estimate to user i, scaled by the power coefficient η_mi.
      — Fully distributed: each AP only needs to know its own ĝ_mi.

  • ZERO-FORCING (ZF) at the NETWORK CONTROLLER (across all APs):

        A_ZF  =  Ĝ* · (ĜᵀĜ*)⁻¹ · P                                    (11)

      where P = diag(√η₁, …, √η_K) and Ĝ is the channel-estimate matrix of
      size M×K. Here η_k (note: only a function of user index k, NOT of m!)
      is the per-user power coefficient. ZF requires matrix inversion of a
      K×K Gram matrix and must be computed centrally at the NC — not
      distributed. This is the price of inter-user interference nulling.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.5  A VERY SHORT NUMERICAL EXAMPLE (rough magnitudes)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

To calibrate intuition for the numbers in the Introduction:

  Scenario (from Experiment 2 in Section VI):
       M = 128 APs, K = 16 users, 2×2 km² urban area,
       maximum AP transmit power ρ_f = 200 mW,
       bandwidth B = 20 MHz,
       COST-Hata path-loss model,
       shadowing std σ_shad = 8 dB.

  Reported 5%-outage rates:
       Small-Cell MF Full Power:         ≈ 0.51 bits/s/Hz
       Cell-Free CB Optimal Max-Min:     ≈ 2.82 bits/s/Hz
       Cell-Free ZF Optimal Max-Min:     ≈ 5.28 bits/s/Hz

  Gains over small-cell full-power baseline:
       CB gain   =  2.82 / 0.51  ≈  5.5×
       ZF gain   =  5.28 / 0.51  ≈ 10.4×

This is where the paper's abstract claims "five- to ten-fold improvement"
come from. In absolute terms:
       At B = 20 MHz:
            5%-outage throughput =  5.28 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz ≈ 106 Mbps
            (cell-free ZF) versus
            0.51 bits/s/Hz × 20 MHz ≈ 10 Mbps (small-cell baseline).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.6  PRACTICE PROBLEM (for the reader)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM. Suppose a cell-free Massive MIMO system has M = 100 APs and K = 10
users. Each AP transmits a signal x_m of the CB form (3), with power
coefficients η_mk such that

                 Σᵢ η_mi · β_mi ≤ 1      ∀m.

Assume:
  • f = 200 mW,
  • For AP m = 1: β_{1,1} = 10⁻⁸, β_{1,k} = 10⁻⁹ for k=2,…,10.
  • η_{1,1} = η₁, η_{1,k} = η for k=2,…,10.

Find the largest η₁ and η satisfying both the per-AP constraint and η₁ = η
(uniform). Then, if we relax η₁ and set η = 0 for k ≥ 2, find the largest η₁.

SOLUTION SKETCH.

  (i) Uniform case: η₁ · β_{1,1} + η · (9 · β_{1,k}) ≤ 1 with η₁ = η = η̄.
       ⟹  η̄ · (10⁻⁸ + 9·10⁻⁹) ≤ 1
       ⟹  η̄ · (1.9 × 10⁻⁸) ≤ 1
       ⟹  η̄ ≤ 5.26 × 10⁷.
      (Units are such that η itself is dimensionless; the physical transmit
      power for user 1 is f · η̄ · β_{1,1} = 200 mW × 5.26×10⁷ × 10⁻⁸ =
      105 mW — i.e., AP 1 spends roughly half its power on user 1.)

 (ii) Focus-on-user-1 case: η_{1,1} · β_{1,1} ≤ 1 ⟹ η_{1,1} ≤ 10⁸. This is
      almost twice the uniform value. But this assumes AP 1 helps only user 1
      and no one else — a policy that would hurt all other users, which
      is why max-min forbids it.

This simple exercise shows why per-AP power constraints *couple* the
individual η_mk across users on the same AP, and why the optimal allocation
is not obvious.

PART 4 — SECTION SUMMARY
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

• CORE IDEA. Cell-Free Massive MIMO replaces large centralized antenna arrays
  with M ≫ K randomly-placed single-antenna APs jointly serving all K users,
  coordinated by a Network Controller (NC).

• TWO LINEAR PRECODERS studied:
     (a) Conjugate Beamforming (CB) — distributed, simple, each AP uses its
         own channel estimate.
     (b) Zero-Forcing (ZF) — centralized, requires K×K matrix inversion at
         the NC, but cancels multi-user interference.

• PERFORMANCE METRIC. Max-min SINR, a fairness-oriented criterion, leading
  to minimum rate R_min and 5%-outage rate R_{5%} as the observed KPIs.

• KEY CONTRIBUTIONS.
    1. Capacity lower bounds for both CB and ZF in cell-free (Theorem 1 and
       Appendix A).
    2. Exact max-min power allocation via bisection + convex feasibility
       check.
    3. Low-complexity heuristic power allocations for CB (full power,
       heuristic fixed-power using exponential fitting, heuristic uniform
       coefficients) in Sections III-B/C/D.
    4. A near-optimal closed-form power allocation for ZF (Eq. 21) derived
       from the perfect-CSI solution (Lemma 2) and proven ε-optimal
       (Proposition 2).
    5. Small-cell baseline with MF and ZF for fair comparison (Section V).

• HEADLINE RESULT. Cell-Free ZF with optimal max-min power control
  achieves ≈ 10× gain in 5%-outage rate over small-cell MF with full power,
  and ≈ 5× gain with CB.

• COUNTERINTUITIVE RESULT. At the max-min optimum for CB, most APs
  transmit at less than full power, and APs close to users transmit with
  smaller power (empirically characterized in Fig. 3).

• LINEAGE. The paper sits directly after:
     [2]  Marzetta 2010        — Massive MIMO foundation,
     [22] Ngo et al. 2017      — "Cell-Free Massive MIMO vs. Small Cells",
  and refines the power-control question left open in [22].

• ASSUMPTIONS CRITICAL TO THE ANALYSIS.
     - TDD + channel reciprocity.
     - Low mobility (no pilot contamination).
     - NC knows all β_mk.
     - MMSE channel estimation from orthogonal uplink pilots.
     - Per-AP (not sum-) transmit-power constraints.

• ROADMAP PROMISED BY THE INTRODUCTION.
     Sec. II  — System model + channel estimation.
     Sec. III — CB: optimal + 3 heuristic power allocations.
     Sec. IV  — ZF: optimal + low-complexity near-optimal allocation.
     Sec. V   — Small-cell systems (MF and ZF), the comparison baseline.
     Sec. VI  — Numerical results.

Everything the rest of the paper does — the Theorems, the Lemmas, the
numerical experiments — is aimed at substantiating the four headline results
declared in this Introduction.
