PART 1 — HIGH-LEVEL / LAYMAN'S EXPLANATION
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Before you can optimize anything, you need to describe the playing field.
Section II does exactly that. It says: "Here's what our wireless world looks
like, here's how signals travel through it, and here's how we figure out what
the channel looks like at any given moment." It does NOT yet talk about
precoding or power allocation — it only sets the stage.

The section has two sub-ideas.

SUB-IDEA #1 — The physical picture.
  Think of a 2 km × 2 km urban area. Scatter M = 128 little radio boxes
  ("access points" or APs) at random — each one roughly the size of a Wi-Fi
  router, each with ONE antenna, each mounted on a lamppost or wall. Throw
  K = 16 cellphone users into the same area. Every user connects to EVERY
  AP. No AP "belongs" to a user and no user "belongs" to an AP — that's why
  the system is called "cell-free". A backhaul wire (fiber, ethernet,
  whatever) connects every AP to a central brain called the Network
  Controller (NC). The NC decides what each AP transmits.

  The signal from AP m to user k has two factors:
     - BIG-SCALE fading β_mk: a slow-moving knob that depends on HOW FAR
       apart they are and what walls/obstacles are in between. It changes
       slowly (like every few seconds), so you can track it easily.
     - SMALL-SCALE fading h_mk: a fast-moving knob caused by multipath
       interference. It changes every few milliseconds and is random
       (Rayleigh fading, in our case).

SUB-IDEA #2 — How do the APs "learn" the channel?
  Think of the channel like the shape of a hallway echoing sound. If you
  want to shoot a ball of sound from one end to the other without hitting
  the walls, you first need to know the shape of the hallway. The APs
  "learn" the channel each coherence block by having the USERS transmit
  short orthogonal tones ("pilot sequences"). The AP listens to the tones,
  knows which user sent which tone (because they're mathematically
  orthogonal to each other), and computes the Minimum-Mean-Square-Error
  (MMSE) estimate of its channel to each user. The estimate is never
  perfect — there's always some residual error — but it's the statistically
  best guess given the measurements.

  One nice thing about having slow-moving users (pedestrians, not cars):
  the channel stays stable for a LONG time, so there's plenty of time slots
  available for pilots. You can give every user a UNIQUE orthogonal tone.
  This kills "pilot contamination" — the situation where two users
  accidentally use the same tone and confuse the AP about whose signal is
  whose. Pilot contamination is the #1 enemy of Massive MIMO, and this
  paper sidesteps it entirely by assumption.

At the end of Section II, the reader holds:
  (i)   a formula for the channel g_mk between each AP m and user k,
  (ii)  a formula for the AP's noisy measurement y_m during pilot time,
  (iii) a formula for the MMSE channel estimate ĝ_mk,
  (iv)  statistics of the estimation error ~g_mk = g_mk − ĝ_mk.

These four pieces are the raw material used by every subsequent section.

PART 2 — DETAILED, TECHNICAL (PhD-LEVEL) EXPLANATION

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1  THE CELL-FREE TOPOLOGY (II-A, paragraph 1)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The authors formalize a network that is a radical departure from cellular:

  ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Definition (paraphrased):                                   │
  │   A cell-free system is a set of M single-antenna APs,      │
  │   randomly dropped over a 2D area, serving K single-antenna │
  │   users simultaneously via a Network Controller (NC). No    │
  │   cell partitions; no user-AP association; every AP serves  │
  │   every user.                                               │
  └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Three details in the formalization deserve emphasis:

(a) M ≫ K. This is the Massive-MIMO flavor. With far more APs than users,
    the joint "array" (the aggregation of all APs) has enough degrees of
    freedom to spatially separate users even with simple linear precoding.
    Think of it as *having K orthogonal bullets* available to fire into a
    dimension-M space.

(b) Single-antenna APs. A deliberate design choice. It implies:
      - Low per-AP hardware cost (no phased array per AP).
      - Distributed-antenna interpretation: M physically separated
        "array elements" that behave, from the user's perspective, like
        one virtual "cell-wide array".
      - Signal processing complexity is shoveled into the NC; APs are
        essentially "dumb" front ends — they upconvert, amplify, transmit.

(c) Random, distributed placement. Distinguishes this from centralized
    Massive MIMO (one massive array) and also from "small cells" (each user
    served by one AP). The randomness of placement is essential for the
    statistical fairness argument — in expectation, every user has a
    nearby AP.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2  PROPAGATION-TIME / CP CONSTRAINT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A subtle but vital sentence in the paper:

  "Size of the overall area is considered small enough that the largest
   difference between propagation time from any two APs to a user is
   smaller than the duration of the OFDM cyclic prefix."

Why does this matter? If AP 1 and AP 128 are both transmitting the same OFDM
symbol to user k, their signals will arrive at slightly different times
because light travels ~3·10⁸ m/s. If the delay spread between "earliest AP
signal" and "latest AP signal" exceeds the OFDM cyclic prefix (CP) length,
the signals can no longer be combined into a single frequency-domain OFDM
demodulation at the user — inter-symbol interference (ISI) appears and
the tidy single-subcarrier model breaks.

Concrete bound: if CP length is T_CP = 4.7 µs (standard LTE short CP), then
the maximum allowed geographic spread between APs (for a common user) is:

       Δd_max  =  c · T_CP  =  (3×10⁸ m/s) · (4.7×10⁻⁶ s)  ≈  1.41 km.

This is precisely why the paper uses a 2 km × 2 km area — it's at the edge
of what CP-length can tolerate. Larger coverage areas would need either
extended-CP OFDM or per-AP timing pre-compensation.

The system operates on a FLAT-FADING per-subcarrier basis. OFDM converts
the wideband channel into many narrowband parallel sub-channels, each flat.
The OFDM subcarrier index is suppressed throughout the paper since every
subcarrier is processed identically.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3  THE CHANNEL MODEL (II-A, Equation 1)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The channel coefficient from AP m to user k is factorized:

       g_mk  =  √(β_mk) · h_mk                                        (1)

Each factor has a distinct physical role:

  β_mk — LARGE-SCALE FADING.
     - Deterministic given geometry + shadowing realization.
     - Captures path loss (function of distance) and log-normal shadowing
       (local terrain / blockage).
     - Changes on the order of seconds (tens of coherence intervals).
     - ASSUMED KNOWN at NC: β_mk is tracked via long-term averaging of
       received signal strengths and conveyed via backhaul.

     The numerical experiments use the COST-Hata model (previewed from
     Section VI):

         10 · log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·log₁₀(d_mk) + X_mk,

     where d_mk is the AP-user distance in km, and X_mk ~ 𝒩(0, σ²_shad)
     with σ_shad = 8 dB is log-normal shadowing. This matches measured
     urban propagation in the 1–2 GHz band.

  h_mk — SMALL-SCALE FADING.
     - h_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1), i.e., zero-mean unit-variance complex Gaussian.
     - i.i.d. across (m, k) — this assumes rich enough scattering that
       any two AP-user pairs experience uncorrelated multipath.
     - Constant over a coherence interval (τ_c symbols), independent
       across coherence intervals.
     - UNKNOWN — this is what needs to be estimated every coherence
       interval.

From (1), g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk), so the channel power E[|g_mk|²] = β_mk.

Channel matrix form: G ∈ ℂ^{M×K}, with [G]_mk = g_mk.
   - Row m is the vector of channels from AP m to all K users.
   - Column k is the vector of channels from all M APs to user k (which we
     often denote g_k in the paper's later sections).

The i.i.d. h_mk assumption yields the convenient property that COLUMNS of
G are "almost orthogonal" in a Massive MIMO sense when M → ∞:

       (g_k)ᴴ · g_j / M  →  β_mk · δ_{kj}   (in probability, heuristically).

This is the SEED of channel hardening, the effect that makes Massive MIMO
work with only statistical CSI at users.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4  CHANNEL RECIPROCITY AND TDD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The paper explicitly assumes TDD (Time-Division Duplex) and channel
reciprocity: the channel coefficient from user k to AP m during uplink is
identical to the coefficient from AP m to user k during downlink.

Why this matters enormously:
  • In FDD (Frequency-Division Duplex), uplink and downlink use DIFFERENT
    frequency bands. The channel at one frequency gives no information
    about the channel at another. To get downlink CSI, the base station
    would have to transmit pilots, have users estimate the channel, and
    feed it BACK — a process that scales as O(M·K) in feedback overhead.
    In Massive MIMO with M = 128, this is a nightmare.

  • In TDD, a single uplink pilot transmission lets the AP directly
    estimate the downlink channel (modulo calibration of the RF chains).
    Pilot overhead scales with K, not M. This is the canonical Marzetta
    argument for TDD Massive MIMO [2].

So: TDD + reciprocity makes the whole paper feasible. In fact, the
computational gymnastics of Sections III-IV hinge on the fact that the NC
can use uplink-pilot-derived estimates ĝ_mk for downlink precoding
without any feedback loop.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.5  MOBILITY AND THE PILOT-CONTAMINATION ESCAPE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

User mobility is capped at 10 km/h (pedestrians). Why this matters for
pilot design:

The coherence time of a channel is approximated by the Jakes' model:

       T_c  ≈  1 / (4 · f_D)  with  f_D = v · f_c / c.

At v = 10 km/h = 2.78 m/s and f_c = 1.9 GHz:
       f_D = (2.78 · 1.9·10⁹) / (3·10⁸) ≈ 17.6 Hz,
       T_c ≈ 1 / (4·17.6)   ≈ 14.2 ms.

For typical LTE OFDM symbols (≈ 71.4 µs), that's roughly 200 OFDM symbols
per coherence block. In the frequency domain, one coherence interval covers
several hundred subcarriers.

Contrast this with a vehicle at 120 km/h:
       f_D ≈ 211 Hz, T_c ≈ 1.18 ms, roughly 16 OFDM symbols.

With 200+ "pilot slots" available per coherence block and only K = 16 users
to serve, you can easily give each user a UNIQUELY ORTHOGONAL pilot. This
kills pilot contamination. If instead you had 16 slots and 16 users, every
slot would need to be reused across geographic regions — causing two
users (one near AP 1, one near AP 128) to transmit the same pilot, leading
AP 1 to "see" the sum of their channels and not be able to distinguish
them. That is pilot contamination, and it fundamentally bounds Massive
MIMO performance (see Marzetta 2010).

Remark 1 in the paper explicitly acknowledges this: the paper's low-
mobility assumption is for *analytical cleanliness* — to isolate the
precoding + power-control question from the pilot assignment question.

Reference [22] (Ngo et al. "Cell-Free vs Small Cells") handles the high-
mobility / pilot-contamination case in detail.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.6  THE UPLINK TRAINING MODEL (II-B)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The TDD protocol is that of Marzetta "How much training is required..."
[23]. Structure per coherence block:

       ┌──────── Coherence block (τ_c symbols) ────────┐
       │                                                │
       │ [ τ pilot symbols ][  τ_c − τ  data symbols ] │
       │     uplink               downlink              │
       └────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

During the pilot phase:
   - All K users simultaneously transmit their pilot sequences.
   - Each pilot sequence ψ_k ∈ ℂ^τ is τ symbols long.
   - Pilots are orthonormal:  ψ_iᴴ · ψ_j = δ_{ij}  (Kronecker delta).
   - The minimum pilot length for K orthogonal sequences is τ ≥ K.
     (In Section VI, the paper uses τ = K.)

The received τ-dimensional signal at AP m is:

       y_m  =  √(ρ_r · τ) · Σᵢ₌₁ᴷ g_mi · ψ_i  +  w_m                  (*)

where:
  • ρ_r is the uplink transmit power per symbol (normalized such that noise
    variance is 1; equivalently, ρ_r is the uplink SNR).
  • √(ρ_r · τ) is the pilot amplitude. The factor √τ comes from coherent
    accumulation of τ pilot samples — the longer the pilot, the higher the
    effective SNR. This is why the authors can credibly claim small
    channel estimation error.
  • w_m ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, I_τ) is τ-dimensional zero-mean complex Gaussian noise at
    AP m.

Note: (*) is NOT numbered in the paper, but it is stated between equations
(1) and the MMSE equation. Treat it as Eq. (*).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.7  MMSE CHANNEL ESTIMATION — DERIVATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The MMSE (Minimum Mean Square Error) estimator of g_mk given y_m is the
conditional expectation:

       ĝ_mk  =  E[g_mk | y_m]

Since (g_mk, y_m) are jointly Gaussian, the MMSE estimator is LINEAR and
given by:

       ĝ_mk  =  E[g_mk · y_mᴴ] · (E[y_m · y_mᴴ])⁻¹ · y_m.

Step 1 — Project onto the k-th pilot direction.
Because the pilots are orthonormal, we can multiply both sides of (*) by
ψ_kᴴ to isolate user k:

       y_{m,k}  :=  ψ_kᴴ · y_m
              =  √(ρ_r · τ) · g_mk + ψ_kᴴ · w_m
              =  √(ρ_r · τ) · g_mk + n_{m,k},

where n_{m,k} = ψ_kᴴ · w_m ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1) (because ψ_k has unit norm, so
projecting unit-covariance noise onto it gives unit-variance scalar noise).

The cross-terms Σ_{i ≠ k} g_mi · (ψ_iᴴ ψ_k) = 0 because of orthogonality.
This is the magic of orthogonal pilots: de-couples the K-dimensional joint
estimation problem into K independent scalar estimation problems.

Step 2 — Scalar MMSE on y_{m,k}.
Now we have a scalar Gaussian observation:
     y_{m,k} = √(ρ_r τ) · g_mk + n_{m,k},    g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk),
                                             n_{m,k} ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1).

The MMSE estimate of g_mk given y_{m,k} is (textbook, e.g., Kay Vol I,
Ch. 11, reference [24]):

       ĝ_mk  =  ( √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk ) / ( 1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk ) · y_{m,k}    (2′)

       ĝ_mk  =  ( √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk ) / ( 1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk ) · ψ_kᴴ y_m

which matches the formula in the paper.

Step 3 — Statistics of ĝ_mk and of the error ~g_mk.
Define γ_mk as the variance of ĝ_mk:

       γ_mk  =  Var(ĝ_mk)
            =  ( ρ_r · τ · β_mk² ) / ( 1 + ρ_r · τ · β_mk )            (γ)

A textbook orthogonality-principle result gives:
       ĝ_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk),
       ~g_mk := g_mk − ĝ_mk,
       ~g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk − γ_mk),
       ĝ_mk ⊥ ~g_mk  (uncorrelated, hence independent since both Gaussian).

This is precisely Eq. (2) of the paper:

       ĝ_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk),  ~g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk − γ_mk).                (2)

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.8  INTERPRETING γ_mk (THE "ESTIMATION SIGNAL POWER")
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The quantity γ_mk is fundamental to every downstream rate formula. Let's
understand its behavior.

Rewrite:
       γ_mk  =  β_mk · [ (ρ_r · τ · β_mk) / (1 + ρ_r · τ · β_mk) ].

The bracketed factor is always ∈ (0, 1). Interpret it as the "channel
estimation quality factor":

  • When ρ_r · τ · β_mk ≫ 1  (strong signal + long pilot + close AP):
           γ_mk ≈ β_mk   (almost perfect estimation).

  • When ρ_r · τ · β_mk ≪ 1  (weak signal + short pilot + far AP):
           γ_mk ≈ ρ_r · τ · β_mk²   (very poor estimation).

Hence APs close to user k have γ_mk ≈ β_mk (their channel is known with
high accuracy) while APs far from user k have γ_mk ≪ β_mk (their channel
is barely estimated at all). This unevenness is one of the reasons why
"most APs should transmit less" becomes optimal in Section III — a far AP
with a noisy estimate is likely to cause more interference than useful
signal, so why shout?

Also note the conservation law:

       β_mk   =    γ_mk   +   (β_mk − γ_mk)
       (total)  (signal part)  (estimation error part).

This is the classical MMSE decomposition.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.9  CONSEQUENCES FOR DOWNSTREAM SECTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section II quietly locks in several facts that will reappear in Sec. III–IV:

  F1. DOWNLINK PRECODING USES ĝ_mk (not g_mk). The NC/APs have NO access
      to the true channel; they only have the estimates. Any rate formula
      that pretends otherwise would be dishonest.

  F2. STATISTICAL CSI AT USERS. Users do NOT know their effective channel
      gain — they only know the *statistics* γ_mk. This is where channel
      hardening steps in: in Massive MIMO, the effective gain concentrates
      on its mean, so receivers can treat the statistical value as if it
      were the instantaneous value. This is the assumption that makes the
      capacity lower bound in Appendix A tight.

  F3. THE ERROR TERM ~g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk − γ_mk) WILL APPEAR IN THE SINR
      DENOMINATOR. Specifically, the T3 term in Appendix A and the J1
      term in Theorem 1 both directly involve E[|~g_mk|²] = β_mk − γ_mk.
      If the authors had ignored estimation error, they would get an
      overly optimistic rate bound.

  F4. ONLY THE β_mk's (not the h_mk's) ARE NEEDED FOR POWER OPTIMIZATION.
      Because the rate expressions (5) and (13) depend only on β_mk and
      γ_mk (the latter a deterministic function of β_mk), the NC can
      do power optimization using only slow-varying large-scale fading
      coefficients. This is crucial for keeping backhaul traffic low:
      power coefficients only need to be updated when β changes, which
      is ~40× slower than h changes.

PART 3 — HISTORICAL / PRIOR DEVELOPMENT
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The tools used in Section II are classical, but they sit atop a particular
historical scaffold worth walking through.

(a) THE √(β) · h FACTORIZATION.
    This two-scale channel decomposition (slow large-scale × fast
    small-scale) was formalized in distributed-antenna-system (DAS)
    literature in the 1990s, tracing back to foundational work by
    Saleh-Valenzuela and to path-loss/shadowing measurement campaigns
    from the 1970s–1990s (Okumura-Hata, COST-231). The factorization is
    *universally* used in cellular modeling.

(b) OFDM + FLAT-FADING-PER-SUBCARRIER ABSTRACTION.
    The principle that OFDM converts a frequency-selective channel into
    a bank of parallel flat sub-channels was published by Weinstein and
    Ebert in 1971 and adopted for 802.11a (1999) and LTE (2009). This
    abstraction is what lets the paper suppress the subcarrier index
    and pretend every subcarrier is an independent SISO-flat problem.

(c) TDD + CHANNEL RECIPROCITY IN MASSIVE MIMO.
    Marzetta's 2010 paper [2] recognized that TDD is not just "nice to
    have" for Massive MIMO — it's *mandatory*. The number of pilots needed
    in FDD scales with the number of base-station antennas (M), which
    would be catastrophic for M = 128. In TDD, it scales only with the
    number of users (K). All cell-free Massive MIMO papers ([21], [22],
    and this one) inherit this architectural constraint.

(d) MMSE CHANNEL ESTIMATION.
    The MMSE estimator for a scalar Gaussian observation y = a·x + n, with
    x, n independent Gaussian, is a staple of estimation theory — see
    Kay Vol I Chapter 11 [24]. The paper directly cites this textbook. The
    derivation we walked through (projection onto the orthogonal pilot
    direction, then scalar MMSE) is the standard recipe used in:
      - Marzetta (2010) [2],
      - Ngo, Larsson, Marzetta "Energy and Spectral Efficiency of Very
        Large Multiuser MIMO Systems" (IEEE Trans. Commun., 2013),
      - Björnson, Hoydis, Sanguinetti "Massive MIMO Networks" monograph
        (2017).

(e) PILOT CONTAMINATION.
    First articulated as a fundamental performance limit of Massive MIMO
    by Marzetta (2010) [2]. The pilot-contamination phenomenon became a
    cottage industry of 2012–2018 literature, with solutions ranging from
    pilot coordination, to pilot-reuse with channel covariance exploitation
    (Yin, Gesbert, Cottatellucci [20]), to blind pilot decontamination
    (Müller et al.). The present paper *escapes* the whole literature by
    assuming enough coherence-time to give every user an orthogonal pilot
    — a luxury only available under low mobility.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.2  DERIVATION OF γ_mk FROM FIRST PRINCIPLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Starting from the scalar observation after pilot projection:

       y_{m,k}  =  √(ρ_r τ) · g_mk  +  n_{m,k},
            g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk),
            n_{m,k} ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1),
            g_mk ⊥ n_{m,k}.

Step 1 — cross-correlation:
       E[g_mk · y_{m,k}ᴴ]  =  E[g_mk · ( √(ρ_r τ) · g_mk + n_{m,k} )ᴴ]
                           =  √(ρ_r τ) · E[|g_mk|²]
                           =  √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk.

Step 2 — auto-correlation of observation:
       E[|y_{m,k}|²]  =  ρ_r · τ · β_mk  +  1.

Step 3 — MMSE coefficient:
       c_mk  =  E[g_mk · y_{m,k}ᴴ] / E[|y_{m,k}|²]
            =  √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk).

Step 4 — MMSE estimate:
       ĝ_mk  =  c_mk · y_{m,k}
            =  [ √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk) ] · ψ_kᴴ y_m.

Step 5 — variance of ĝ_mk:
       Var(ĝ_mk)  =  |c_mk|² · Var(y_{m,k})
                  =  [ρ_r τ · β_mk² / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk)²] · (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk)
                  =  ρ_r · τ · β_mk² / (1 + ρ_r · τ · β_mk)
                  ≡  γ_mk.          ✓

Step 6 — variance of error:
       Var(~g_mk) = Var(g_mk) − Var(ĝ_mk)   (orthogonality principle)
                  = β_mk − γ_mk
                  = β_mk · [1 − (ρ_r τ β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk))]
                  = β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk).                        (♦)

The compact form (♦) is useful in later calculations and is equivalent to
β_mk − γ_mk. The paper uses both forms interchangeably.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.3  THE ORTHOGONALITY PRINCIPLE (why ĝ_mk ⊥ ~g_mk)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For a linear MMSE estimator, the error is orthogonal (uncorrelated) to the
estimate. Proof sketch:

       E[~g_mk · ĝ_mkᴴ]
     = E[(g_mk − ĝ_mk) · ĝ_mkᴴ]
     = E[g_mk · ĝ_mkᴴ] − E[|ĝ_mk|²]
     = E[g_mk · c_mkᴴ · y_{m,k}ᴴ] − γ_mk
     = c_mkᴴ · E[g_mk · y_{m,k}ᴴ] − γ_mk
     = c_mkᴴ · (√(ρ_r τ) · β_mk) − γ_mk
     = [ √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk) ] · √(ρ_r τ) · β_mk − γ_mk
     = ρ_r · τ · β_mk² / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk) − γ_mk
     = γ_mk − γ_mk  =  0.    ✓

Since both ĝ_mk and ~g_mk are Gaussian (both are linear combinations of
jointly Gaussian variables), uncorrelated ⟹ independent. This is used
repeatedly in Appendix A of the paper when computing the variances of
T₀, T₁, T₂, T₃.

PART 5 — NUMERICAL EXAMPLE
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.1  EXAMPLE 1: γ_mk at typical parameter values
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Parameters from Section VI:
   ρ_r   =  200 mW (uplink max)
   noise variance at AP =  σ²_w  =  k_B · T · B · NF
         =  (1.38·10⁻²³) · 290 · (20·10⁶) · 10^(9/10)
         ≈  6.37 · 10⁻¹⁴ W
         ≈  −131.96 dBm.

Normalized uplink power (the ρ_r used in equations is
     ρ̃_r = ρ_r(W) / σ²_w):
     ρ̃_r = (200·10⁻³) / (6.37·10⁻¹⁴) ≈ 3.14·10¹² ≈ 124.96 dB.

Pilot length τ = K = 16.

Case A — AP close to the user (d_mk = 50 m = 0.05 km):
   Using the COST-Hata model:
     10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·log₁₀(0.05) + X_mk
                    = −136 − 35·(−1.301)
                    = −136 + 45.5
                    = −90.5 dB   (ignoring shadowing).
     β_mk ≈ 10⁻⁹·⁰⁵ ≈ 8.91 · 10⁻¹⁰.

   Now compute ρ̃_r · τ · β_mk:
     = 3.14·10¹² · 16 · 8.91·10⁻¹⁰
     ≈ 4.48·10⁴.

   This is ≫ 1, so γ_mk is almost β_mk:
     γ_mk / β_mk ≈ 4.48·10⁴ / (1 + 4.48·10⁴) ≈ 0.9999777.
   Error fraction:
     (β_mk − γ_mk) / β_mk ≈ 2.23·10⁻⁵.
   Interpretation: AP is close, pilot SNR is enormous, estimation is
   near-perfect.

Case B — AP far from the user (d_mk = 1 km):
     10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·log₁₀(1) + 0 = −136 dB.
     β_mk ≈ 10⁻¹³·⁶ ≈ 2.51 · 10⁻¹⁴.
     ρ̃_r · τ · β_mk ≈ 3.14·10¹² · 16 · 2.51·10⁻¹⁴ ≈ 1.26.

   This is near unity — the "transition zone":
     γ_mk / β_mk ≈ 1.26 / 2.26 ≈ 0.558.
   About half the channel power can be estimated, half remains as error.

Case C — AP very far (d_mk = 2 km):
     10·log₁₀(β_mk) = −136 − 35·log₁₀(2) ≈ −146.5 dB,
     β_mk ≈ 2.24 · 10⁻¹⁵,
     ρ̃_r · τ · β_mk ≈ 0.112.
   γ_mk / β_mk ≈ 0.112 / 1.112 ≈ 0.1006.
   Only ~10% of the (already tiny) channel power can be estimated —
   essentially useless.

These numbers explain why in the cell-free power-optimization problem,
only a FEW dominant nearby APs really contribute to each user; the
far-away APs have effectively no useful channel state.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5.2  PRACTICE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM 1. Consider AP m and user k with:
     β_mk  =  10⁻¹⁰   (i.e., a path-loss of 100 dB),
     ρ_r (normalized) = 10⁹   (90 dB uplink SNR),
     τ = 20.

Find γ_mk, β_mk − γ_mk, and the fraction of channel power captured by
the estimate.

ANSWER.
     ρ_r τ β_mk = 10⁹ · 20 · 10⁻¹⁰ = 2.0.
     γ_mk = (2.0 · 10⁻¹⁰) / (1 + 2.0) = 10⁻¹⁰ · (2/3) ≈ 6.67 · 10⁻¹¹.
     β_mk − γ_mk = 10⁻¹⁰ − 6.67 · 10⁻¹¹ = 3.33 · 10⁻¹¹.
     Fraction captured: γ_mk / β_mk = 2/3 ≈ 66.7%.


PROBLEM 2 (harder). Given β_mk and target accuracy η (i.e., you want
γ_mk / β_mk ≥ 1 − η), find the minimum required pilot length τ_min in
terms of ρ_r, β_mk, and η.

ANSWER.
     Require:   (ρ_r τ β_mk) / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk)  ≥  1 − η
           ⟺   ρ_r τ β_mk  ≥  (1 − η)(1 + ρ_r τ β_mk)
           ⟺   ρ_r τ β_mk  ≥  (1 − η) + (1 − η) · ρ_r τ β_mk
           ⟺   η · ρ_r τ β_mk  ≥  (1 − η)
           ⟺   τ  ≥  (1 − η) / (η · ρ_r · β_mk).

     Example: η = 0.05 (5% error), ρ_r = 10¹², β_mk = 10⁻¹³
              τ_min  =  0.95 / (0.05 · 10¹² · 10⁻¹³) = 0.95 / 0.005 = 190.
     So you would need 190 orthogonal pilot symbols — consistent with
     the intuition that far-APs need long pilots for good estimation.

This shows why increasing the uplink power ρ_r (or the coherence interval
τ_c that permits larger τ) is so effective at reducing estimation error.

PART 6 — SECTION SUMMARY
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

SYSTEM MODEL (II-A):
  • M ≫ K single-antenna APs, K single-antenna users, area ≤ 2×2 km²
    (constrained by OFDM CP length).
  • Flat-fading per subcarrier (thanks to OFDM).
  • Channel:  g_mk  =  √(β_mk) · h_mk,  with h_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, 1).
  • TDD operation + channel reciprocity → uplink pilots give downlink CSI
    with overhead O(K), not O(M).
  • NC knows all β_mk (slowly varying, trackable).
  • User mobility ≤ 10 km/h → large coherence interval → orthogonal
    pilots for every user → no pilot contamination.

CHANNEL ESTIMATION (II-B):
  • K users transmit mutually orthonormal pilots ψ_k ∈ ℂ^τ (τ ≥ K).
  • Each AP receives y_m = √(ρ_r τ) Σᵢ g_mi ψ_i + w_m.
  • MMSE estimator (Eq. 2):
       ĝ_mk = [√(ρ_r τ) · β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk)] · ψ_kᴴ y_m.
  • Estimate and error distributions:
       ĝ_mk  ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk),
       ~g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk − γ_mk),
    with
       γ_mk = ρ_r τ · β_mk² / (1 + ρ_r τ · β_mk).
  • ĝ_mk and ~g_mk are uncorrelated (and, being Gaussian, independent).
  • The "estimation quality factor" γ_mk / β_mk = ρ_r τ β_mk / (1+ρ_r τ β_mk)
    goes to 1 for nearby APs and to 0 for distant APs.

DESIGN TAKE-AWAYS CARRIED FORWARD:
  1. Rate bounds in Sections III and IV depend only on β_mk and γ_mk,
     i.e., only on slowly-varying large-scale fading. Power coefficients
     can be recomputed infrequently → very low backhaul traffic.
  2. The channel estimation error will materially affect the SINR
     expressions via terms like Σᵢ η_mi β_mk γ_mi or Σᵢ η_i ν_{ki}
     (see Eq. 5 and Eq. 13 in the paper).
  3. The low-mobility assumption is a deliberate simplification to
     isolate the power-control problem from pilot-contamination.
     Sections III and IV would need modification in a high-mobility
     regime (see Ngo et al. [22] for that variant).

EQUATIONS INTRODUCED IN SECTION II (that will be reused):
  (1)   g_mk = √(β_mk) · h_mk
  (*)   y_m = √(ρ_r τ) Σᵢ g_mi ψ_i + w_m    (uplink pilot received signal)
  (MMSE) ĝ_mk = [√(ρ_r τ) β_mk / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk)] · ψ_kᴴ y_m
  (2)   ĝ_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, γ_mk),  ~g_mk ~ 𝒞𝒩(0, β_mk − γ_mk)
  (γ)   γ_mk = ρ_r τ β_mk² / (1 + ρ_r τ β_mk)
